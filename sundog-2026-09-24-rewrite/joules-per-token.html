Count the Hours Between the Tokens

A local AI machine spends most of its day waiting. Joules per token measures the busy minutes; your electricity meter counts everything. Here is how to measure what matters.

By Sundog

Rewritten 24 September 2026

For anyone running a model on hardware they pay the power bill for · Arithmetic only, no special equipment needed to follow along

A local model might answer questions for two hours a day. For the other twenty-two, it waits. Nearly every efficiency number you will read about AI measures the two hours.

That is not a flaw in those numbers. Joules per token, the energy spent on each word fragment the model writes, is a good way to compare how efficiently two setups work while they are working. It just answers a narrower question than the one your electricity meter asks. Before comparing setups, decide which question you are asking, and which equipment and which hours belong inside the measurement.

Power is a rate; energy is what you pay for

Power, in watts, is how fast a machine is using energy at a given moment. Energy is that rate accumulated over time. One watt sustained for one hour is one watt-hour (Wh), which is 3,600 joules (J). Your utility bills you for energy, usually in kilowatt-hours (kWh).

The distinction matters because a machine can draw more power and still use less energy, if it finishes much sooner and then drops to a low idle draw. It can also draw very little power and still use a lot of energy, if it does so all day.

One ordinary day, calculated

Here is an invented but plausible day. A whole-machine meter records 80 Wh during two hours of active use. The machine then stays on and ready for 22 hours, averaging 20 W.

Waiting accounts for about 85 percent of this day's energy. At the 2025 U.S. average residential price of 17.3 cents per kWh, the year comes to about $33. [1]

This does not mean the machine should be switched off. Being ready to answer instantly may be exactly the service you want. It means a benchmark of the active interval cannot tell you what the machine costs to run, and that cutting active energy by 30 percent, 24 Wh, would change this day's total by less than 5 percent.

The boundary decides the number

A 2025 study from Google makes the same point at a very different scale. The authors measured the energy of the median text prompt to Gemini Apps, counting the serving machines, spare capacity and data-center overhead, and got 0.24 Wh. The AI accelerators themselves accounted for 0.14 Wh of that. The rest went to the host processors and memory (0.06 Wh), idle machines kept ready for traffic spikes (0.02 Wh), and cooling and power conversion in the data center (0.02 Wh). [2]

The paper also computed the figure the narrower way, counting only accelerator energy and only in its most efficient data centers. That gave 0.10 Wh, less than half. Same service, same company: what they counted, and which data centers they sampled, more than halved the answer. [2]

At home the same choice appears in smaller form. NVIDIA's nvidia-smi reports power.draw, which on recent GPUs is an average over the last second, along with power.draw.average and power.draw.instant. All three cover the graphics board only, to within about 5 W. [3] A plug-in meter at the wall also sees the processor, memory, storage, fans and the power supply's conversion losses. Either works if you compare card readings with card readings and wall readings with wall readings.

What joules per token measures, and what it hides

For a defined interval:

J/token = energy used during the interval (J) ÷ tokens generated in that interval

If a request uses 1,800 J while generating 1,000 tokens, that is 1.8 J/token.

Published figures are rarely comparable with a home setup, because they depend so heavily on how many requests share the hardware. Samsi and colleagues measured about 3 to 4 J per output token for LLaMA 65B, counting GPU energy only, with the model split across 8 to 32 V100 GPUs working on 64 to 512 prompts at once. [4] A server handling many requests at once spreads its power draw across all their tokens; a home machine answering one person does not. [5]

The ML.ENERGY benchmark, from Chung and colleagues, adds two cautions. It reports energy per response rather than per token, because a model that writes longer answers spends more energy per answer even at the same cost per token. And it found that estimating energy from a GPU's rated power, instead of measuring it, overstated the true figure by up to 4.1 times. During token generation, the GPU is mostly waiting on memory and draws far less than its maximum. [5]

When a task has a pass or fail check, the fairest measure counts failures too:

Energy per success = energy for all attempts, including failures ÷ successful tasks

Cheaper per token can fail the job

Quantized models, which store their numbers in 8 or 4 bits instead of 16, are often described as the efficient choice, and sometimes they are. Shi and Ding measured eleven quantization methods on Llama-2 and CodeLlama models served with TensorRT-LLM on NVIDIA H100 and A100 GPUs, tracking latency, energy and quality together. No method won all three. In one set of experiments, quantization improved energy per token by up to 30 percent. In others it did the opposite: on a coding benchmark, two quantized configurations used more energy than the unquantized model, and quantizing the KV cache, the model's stored notes on the conversation, generally raised energy in their setup. [6]

Their provisioning experiment shows the trap most clearly. Planning a large deployment purely for energy produced 0.062 J/token using 24 GPUs, against 0.128 J/token for the unquantized 16-bit plan using 45. It looked like half the energy. But the energy-first plan met its service targets, which included answer quality, only 38.6 percent of the time, against 100 percent for the 16-bit plan. The failure was quality: on the most common kind of chat request, the score fell from 60 to 50.48, below the required 55. [6] The cheapest joule per token belonged to a system that was not doing the job. At home, the same trap is a more heavily quantized model that answers faster but fails your task more often, and energy per success is the measure that catches it.

The authors also found that the older A100 delivered larger energy savings than the H100 in some moderate-load cases, even though the H100 was faster. [6] Speed, precision and energy are separate results, so measure all three on the same workload before choosing.

The fast machine wins the job and can still lose the hour

Here is the tradeoff in one calculation. The numbers are invented to make the mechanism visible.

Two hypothetical setups
SetupPower while workingTime per jobEnergy per jobIdle power
Fast300 W10 s3,000 J40 W
Frugal150 W30 s4,500 J12 W

Per job, Fast wins easily. Now run 20 jobs in an hour and let each machine idle for the rest of it:

Fast:   20 × 3,000 J + 40 W × (3,600 − 200) s = 196,000 J ≈ 54 Wh
Frugal: 20 × 4,500 J + 12 W × (3,600 − 600) s = 126,000 J ≈ 35 Wh

Frugal uses about a third less energy over the hour. To find where the two cross, start from an idle hour: 144,000 J for Fast and 43,200 J for Frugal. Each Fast job adds 2,600 J (3,000 J of work minus the 400 J of idling it replaces); each Frugal job adds 4,140 J. The totals meet at 100,800 ÷ 1,540 ≈ 65 jobs an hour, roughly one a minute. Below that, idle draw decides the winner. Your own crossover depends on your numbers, but the shape is general. The busier the machine, the more working efficiency matters; the quieter it is, the more waiting does.

What you can actually change

The floor. The computer's own idle and sleep behavior sets the energy for every waiting hour, and usually matters most for a machine serving one household.

Whether the model stays loaded. Unloading an Ollama model, with ollama stop or a keep_alive of 0, frees its memory but does not put the computer to sleep, so measure what the machine actually draws afterward rather than assuming. [7] Find What's Using Your DGX Spark's Memory covers how long models stay loaded and how to see it.

How hard the GPU works. Capping a GPU's power is a knob you do have. In Samsi's tests on four A100s serving LLaMA 65B, 64 prompts at a time, lowering the cap from 250 W to 175 W cut total energy by 23.21 percent and slowed inference by 6.7 percent. [4] Providers see the same curve at larger scale: ML.ENERGY found that a chatbot provider choosing among serving configurations could accept 77 milliseconds per output token instead of the fastest possible and cut energy per response by 44 percent. [5] A one-person machine cannot copy that exact result, but the direction holds: if nobody is waiting on the answer, slightly slower can be much cheaper.

[OPINION] I would optimize for useful work per day, not per token. Joules per token is the right tool for comparing two setups while they work. The schedule decides whether that is where your energy goes, and for most machines serving one household, it is not.

Take four measurements with a wall meter

Put a plug-in energy meter between the wall and the machine. Record one full day of normal use, one hour idle with the model loaded, and one hour idle with it unloaded. Then read the meter's total, run a representative task ten times, read it again, and divide the difference by the number of successful runs. Those four numbers will tell you which change is worth making.
