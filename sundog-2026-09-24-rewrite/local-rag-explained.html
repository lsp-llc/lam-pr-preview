The Nearest Passage Is Not Always the Right One

Embeddings cannot tell whether a passage is current, and a single vector cannot always return every right answer. Here is the math behind the second problem and a retrieval stack that handles both.

By Sundog

Rewritten 24 September 2026

For people building or using retrieval over their own documents · Includes the math, explained step by step

Your local assistant says the maintenance window starts Friday at 6 p.m. It quotes the notice word for word. The notice is real, relevant and clearly written. It was also replaced last week by a notice that says 8 p.m.

Nothing in that answer is a hallucination. The model used exactly the evidence it was given. The failure happened one step earlier. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) means searching your documents for passages related to a question and pasting the best matches into the model's prompt, and this search handed over the wrong passage. When a RAG answer is wrong, check what the search returned before you change anything else.

What "similar" means to an embedding model

Documents are first split into passages, usually called chunks. An embedding model turns each chunk into a list of numbers, a vector, arranged so that texts about similar things land near each other. At question time, the question gets a vector too, and retrieval keeps the chunks whose vectors are closest. The usual measure of closeness is cosine similarity:

cos(q, p) = (q · p) / (|q| × |p|)

Here q is the question's vector and p a passage's. The dot product q · p multiplies the two vectors entry by entry and adds up the results, and |q| is a vector's length. The score depends only on the angle between the vectors, not on their lengths; a value near 1 means they point the same way. [1]

Now look at the two notices. Maintenance begins Friday at 6 p.m. and Maintenance begins Friday at 8 p.m. are about the same subject, share almost every word and differ by one digit. Any reasonable embedding model will place them almost on top of each other. That is the model doing its job. Its job is to measure what a text is about. Whether the text is current, authoritative, complete or allowed is not part of that measurement.

An embedding score answers "how much does this passage resemble the question?" It cannot answer "is this passage true today?" Every reliable retrieval system adds something that can.

Fast comparison, careful comparison

There are two basic ways a model can judge whether a passage answers a question. A bi-encoder reads the question and the passage separately, turns each into a vector, and compares the vectors. A cross-encoder reads the question and passage together, so every word of one can be weighed against every word of the other, and outputs a relevance score.

The cross-encoder is usually more accurate, and the Sentence-BERT paper shows why it is too slow to use on a whole collection. To find the most similar pair among 10,000 sentences, a cross-encoder has to read every pair: 10,000 × 9,999 ÷ 2, about 50 million passes, which the authors estimated at roughly 65 hours on a V100 GPU. Sentence-BERT embeds all 10,000 sentences in about five seconds in total, and the 50 million comparisons become cheap vector arithmetic taking about 0.01 seconds. [1]

That arithmetic explains the shape of almost every serious retrieval system. A fast method pulls a few dozen candidates from the whole collection. A careful reranker, typically a cross-encoder, reorders just those few dozen. In Nogueira and Cho's early experiment on the MS MARCO passage benchmark, a BERT reranker applied to keyword-search candidates raised MRR@10, a score that rewards putting the first correct passage near the top, from 16.7 to 36.5 on the development set. [2] Because a reranker reads the question and passage together, it can connect a question that asks for the latest version with a passage that states its date. A bi-encoder has already compressed both away.

Some answers cannot be reached with one vector

A paper from Weller and colleagues at Google DeepMind and Johns Hopkins, published at ICLR 2026, found a deeper limit. It is my favorite result in this area because you can draw it.

Real embeddings have hundreds or thousands of dimensions, which allows vastly more combinations, but the paper proves the ceiling never disappears as long as right answers must outscore wrong ones by some margin. With n documents there are C(n, k) = n! ÷ (k! (n − k)!) possible sets of k results: 6 pairs among the 4 documents in the drawing, and about 1.25 billion pairs among 50,000 documents. The number of sets that queries can single out has a ceiling set by the embedding's dimension, and adding documents never raises it. Once a collection is big enough, some top-k sets cannot be returned by any query at all. [3]

To see how soon the ceiling arrives, the authors ran a best case. They skipped language entirely and optimized the vectors directly against the answers, then found the largest collection for which every pair of documents could still be retrieved as a top two. They measured dimensions 4 through 45 and fitted a cubic curve. Extrapolated, the ceiling is about 500,000 documents at 512 dimensions, 4 million at 1,024 and 250 million at 4,096. A real embedding model, which has to learn from text, cannot beat this best case, and the next experiment shows how far short it falls. [3]

They built LIMIT, a deliberately simple dataset: 50,000 documents describing what people like, and 1,000 questions of the form "Who likes X?", each with exactly two correct answers. Every answer pair comes from the same 46 people, so the questions demand 1,000 of the 1,035 possible pairs among them. The best single-vector embedding model they tested found only 18.9 percent of the right people within its top 100 results. A multi-vector model, which keeps one vector per token instead of one per document, reached 54.8 percent. BM25, a keyword-scoring method from the 1990s, reached 93.6 percent, because matching exact words behaves like a space with one dimension per word. It is not a cure-all: on a version of the test rephrased with synonyms, BM25 lost more than 89 percent of its score. And a large language model that read the documents and questions directly solved the 46-person version completely. [3]

The counting argument, for readers who want it

Suppose every document and query is a unit vector in d dimensions, and say a query realizes a set S of k documents if every document in S outscores every document outside it by at least 2γ. Each realizable set needs its own query, and those queries must sit a minimum distance apart. Only about (1 + 1/γ)d points fit on a d-dimensional sphere at that spacing. The paper's bound is C(n, k) ≤ (1 + 1/γ)d, which rearranges to d ≥ log C(n, k) ÷ log(1 + 1/γ). C(n, k) keeps rising as the collection grows, while (1 + 1/γ)d stays fixed. With γ = 0.1 and a million documents, the bound calls for at least 12 dimensions to make every top-2 set reachable, 52 for every top-10 set and 425 for every top-100 set. These are minimums, and loose ones: in the authors' experiments, even language-free vectors needed about 18 dimensions to handle every pair among 100 documents, where the bound asks for 4. [3]

The practical lesson: when a question asks for a combination, such as "both of these," "all of these except that," "how many," or "which ones like X," do not assume the nearest few chunks form the complete answer. Pull more candidates and let something that can reason about the combination decide: a reranker, a metadata filter, a keyword match or a structured query.

Keyword search is not obsolete; it covers a different blind spot

BM25 scores a passage by how often it contains the question's words, weighted so rare words count more and long passages do not win just by being long. [4] It knows nothing about meaning. It is excellent at exactly the things embeddings smear: error codes, part numbers, names, version strings, and exact numbers that appear in the question.

Running both searches raises the question of how to merge two rankings whose scores are on different scales. The standard answer is reciprocal rank fusion (RRF), from Cormack, Clarke and Büttcher. It ignores the scores and uses only each passage's rank in each list:

RRF(p) = Σ over rankers of 1 / (k + rank(p)),   with k = 60

This k is a smoothing constant, unrelated to the k in "top k." The authors fixed it at 60 during a pilot study and found the exact value was not critical. In their TREC experiments, the fused ranking beat both older fusion methods and the best single system by 4 to 5 percent on average. [5]

A worked example, with made-up ranks. Passage A is first in the keyword list and tenth in the embedding list. Passage B is third and second.

A: 1/(60 + 1) + 1/(60 + 10) = 0.01639 + 0.01429 = 0.03068
B: 1/(60 + 3) + 1/(60 + 2)  = 0.01587 + 0.01613 = 0.03200

B wins. Without the 60, A would score 1/1 + 1/10 = 1.10 and B only 1/3 + 1/2 ≈ 0.83, and A would win on the strength of a single first place. With it, first and third place count almost the same (0.0164 against 0.0159), so a passage that both methods like beats one that only one method loves. That is the behavior you want when each method has blind spots.

Anthropic's 2024 write-up on what it calls contextual retrieval puts numbers on stacking these stages. Their measure was the share of needed chunks missing from the top 20 results. With ordinary embeddings, 5.7 percent were missing. Prepending a short, model-written note about each chunk's place in its document before embedding it cut that to 3.7 percent. Adding BM25 over the same annotated chunks cut it to 2.9 percent, and adding a reranker to 1.9 percent, a two-thirds reduction overall. [6] Those figures come from their collections and models, but the ordering matches the reasoning above: each stage covers a different blind spot.

The 6 p.m. problem needs a rule, not a better score

None of this fixes the notices. Both contain the right words, both mean nearly the same thing, and even a reranker only helps if the question and the passages make the date visible. The dependable fix is to store what the system needs to know as data: a status field of current or superseded, an effective date, a supersedes link. Filter out superseded documents before ranking. Similarity finds the topic. The rule decides which version applies.

Relationships work the same way. A knowledge graph stores facts extracted from documents as connected entries, such as (Company A, supplies, Company B). HybridRAG, from researchers at BlackRock and NVIDIA, combined vector search with a graph built from 50 company earnings-call transcripts and tested 400 analyst questions. The graph did better on questions about named entities and specific extracted facts; vector search did better on open-ended questions and on questions naming no entity at all. The hybrid tied the graph on faithfulness and led on answer relevance, but its context precision, roughly the share of retrieved text that was relevant, fell to 0.79, below vector search alone at 0.84 and the graph alone at 0.96. Combining sources also combined their noise. All three scores came from a language model acting as judge. [7]

Then hand the model less, and put the best first

It is tempting to send twenty passages so the right one is surely in there. Research on how models read long inputs argues against it. In "Lost in the Middle," Liu and colleagues moved the one relevant document around within a set of distractors and found a U-shaped curve: models used information best at the beginning or end of their input and worst in the middle. With 20 documents, GPT-3.5-Turbo answered 75.8 percent of questions correctly when the relevant document came first and 63.2 percent when it came last, but only 53.8 percent when it sat in the middle. Asked the same questions with no documents at all, it scored 56.1 percent. Buried in the middle of 19 distractors, the right document left this 2023 model worse off than having no documents at all. [8]

There is a local cost too. Every passage you pass along takes room in the chat model's context and in its KV cache, the memory it keeps for the conversation, which Why a Longer Chat Needs More Memory shows how to price. On your own machine, the embedding model and any reranker also share memory and processor time with the chat model. So finish by cutting the list down: rerank, keep the few passages that matter, put the strongest one first, and remove superseded versions entirely rather than trusting the model to notice the date.

Test retrieval on its own, before you blame the model

Write ten questions you know the answers to. For each one, list the exact passages a correct answer needs. Include some questions with a tempting outdated passage, some that need two passages, and at least one the documents cannot answer; for that one, check that the final answer admits the documents do not cover it. Then run only the retrieval step and record what comes back.

The simplest score is recall at k: of the passages a question needs, what fraction appear in the top k results?

Recall@k = required passages found in the top k ÷ required passages

A question that needs two passages, with only one in the top five, scores 1 ÷ 2 = 50 percent at k = 5. The pattern of misses tells you what to change:

Reading a retrieval failure
What you seeWhat to change first
The right passage never appears.How documents are split into chunks, the embedding model, adding keyword search, or the number of candidates.
Both notices appear; the old one ranks first.A status or date filter. A better similarity score will not fix this.
Each needed fact is in a different passage and only one comes back.More candidates plus a reranker, or a structured query for combinations.
The right passages arrive at the top and the answer is still wrong.Now look at the prompt and the model.

[OPINION] For a personal or small-team archive, I would start with keyword search and a good embedding model fused with RRF, a status and date filter, and a small reranker over the top few dozen results. That stack is boring, inspectable and covers the blind spots each part has alone. I would add a knowledge graph only after the test above shows failures on relationship questions, because a graph is one more thing that can go stale.

Give every document a status

Before tuning anything else, add a status and an effective date to each document and filter out superseded ones at query time. It is the cheapest change on this page, and the only one that fixes the 6 p.m. answer.
