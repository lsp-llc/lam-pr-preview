Count the Hours Between the Tokens

Use a whole-day example to distinguish power from energy and compare useful work, idle time and model unloading.

By SundogRevised 7 September 2026

Run local AI deliberately

A local model works for ten minutes and waits for the rest of the evening. Its generation benchmark describes the busy part. The electricity meter keeps counting afterward. I want both parts of the day in the comparison.

Start with two units. Power, measured in watts (W), is the rate of energy use. Energy, measured here in watt-hours (Wh), accumulates over time. A steady 20 W for 22 hours uses 440 Wh, or 0.440 kWh. You can have an efficient burst of generation inside an expensive day of waiting.

Count a whole day before choosing a metric

Suppose a whole-machine meter records 80 Wh during two active hours. The machine then waits for 22 hours at an average of 20 W. These numbers are hypothetical, not measurements of a particular GPU or Spark.

In this example, waiting uses about 85% of the energy. Someone may still need the machine available throughout that time. It tells you which part of its schedule is worth measuring before optimizing a few seconds of generation.

[OPINION] I would measure a quiet day as carefully as a busy one. An always-available assistant can be a good choice. I want to see the cost of that availability instead of hiding it behind the best burst of output.

Choose what the meter is counting

A GPU telemetry reading and a wall reading cover different equipment. The wall can include the host, memory, fans and conversion losses as well as the GPU. A supply's rating specifies capacity. To describe a particular inference run, measure its consumption. NVIDIA's RTX 4090 specifications and Apple's Mac Studio power information describe different boundaries and conditions. [1] [2]

A practical first record can use a cumulative energy reading. Note the start value and time, perform a defined workload, then note the end value and time. Subtract the readings. Repeat over a representative waiting interval. Keep the same equipment inside the boundary. This is a proposed measurement method; no meter or platform was tested for this article.

Use joules per token for a matching interval

A joule is another unit of energy; 1 Wh equals 3,600 J. For a defined generation interval, divide its measured energy by the number of tokens generated during that same interval:

J/token = energy in joules ÷ generated tokens

Hypothetical arithmetic: 1,800 J ÷ 1,000 generated tokens = 1.8 J/token.

If the numerator includes loading or prompt processing, label it accordingly. If it includes a whole day of waiting, the result is a different metric from active decode energy per token. A ratio with a tiny amount of output can be dominated by availability rather than generation. The Watt Counts study's stated instrumentation and workload boundaries are part of reading its results. [3]

A faster run need not use less power at every instant. It can still use less energy if the shorter duration outweighs the higher rate. Conversely, reducing instantaneous power can lengthen the work. A study of inference optimizations on A6000 and A100 hardware reports results tied to its models and workload conditions; a smaller file alone does not settle the power or energy comparison. [4]

Read a published result without turning it into a hardware rule

Watt Counts reports an RTX 4090 result of 0.490263 J/token for upstage/SOLAR-10.7B-Instruct-v1.0. The paper treats that result as an outlier; its setup uses vLLM and NVML sampling at 10 Hz. It is not a local Q4 Llama or DGX Spark measurement. Preserve those conditions rather than assigning the number to another model or whole-machine boundary. [3]

I have not tested that model's energy use or reproduced the paper's result. Its role here is to show what a precise number needs beside it: model, runtime, instrumentation, workload and an account of unusual results.

Unloading and sleeping are different changes

In Ollama, ollama ps lists loaded models. On a machine you control, ollama stop MODEL_NAME unloads the named model; replace MODEL_NAME with its actual listed name. The API also exposes keep_alive to control residency. Unloading releases the model's memory allocation. The host remains awake. [5]

Measure what the machine does afterward. A shared service may still be busy, and another request may load the model again. If you test sleep instead, count the transition and the wait for the next usable answer. Keep the result tied to your platform rather than treating unloading, sleeping and powering off as interchangeable.

For the earlier daily example, suppose a different waiting state averaged 2 W for the same 22 hours. Its waiting energy would be 44 Wh, and the day's subtotal would be 124 Wh. The calculated difference is 396 Wh, before any additional transition energy. The invented 2 W assumption lets us compare the arithmetic; actual sleep power needs a measurement. A measured transition and your availability requirement decide whether that saving is attainable or useful.

Do not add idle energy twice. If your 24-hour reading already includes the waiting period, adding a separately estimated 22 hours counts it again. If you subtract an idle baseline to isolate incremental workload energy, label that result and keep the raw total available.

Keep one ordinary day's record

Measure the work and the waiting inside one boundary. Then try one change: a shorter residency period, a planned sleep interval or a different schedule. Compare completed work, total energy and the delay when you need the machine again. For separating load, prompt processing and generation, continue to the response-time article.

The hours between useful work belong in the decision. Once they are visible, you can choose what deserves to stay awake.
