Review ten new Sundog articles

Local AI Models · September 2026 · research-based editorial previews

This is the new ten-article collection. Each article has its own hero, worked example and linked sources. The examples are educational; the collection does not report newly run local-model experiments.

Open the illustrated collection

  1. When Yesterday’s Wrong Answer Becomes Today’s Evidence

    Follow how an early guess can survive later corrections, then separate the current facts from the conversation that accumulated around them.

  2. A Procedure Needs More Than a Clever Prompt

    Compare putting a workflow in context, enforcing its transitions in code, and teaching behavior through an adapter. They preserve different kinds of control.

  3. When to Turn Thinking Off

    Use reasoning research to decide which tasks deserve extra tokens, and why shortening a trace is different from teaching a model when to stop.

  4. More Agents Can Make the Answer Worse

    Distinguish independent attempts, checking, and debate. Shared mistakes and persuasion can consume a budget without adding reliable evidence.

  5. The Missing Number That More Thinking Cannot Find

    Learn to distinguish a difficult question from an underspecified one, and give an assistant a useful way to ask for the information it lacks.

  6. A Better Math Score Doesn’t Promise a Better Assistant

    Separate specialization, transfer, and sample coverage before choosing a reasoning checkpoint for mixed everyday work.

  7. Check the Candidate Before You Ask for Another Answer

    Turn a plausible answer into explicit constraints, understand the limits of self-checking, and choose where an independent verifier earns its cost.

  8. Your Agent Can Lose Track of the World Before It Breaks a Rule

    Separate remembering the current state, predicting an action’s effect, and choosing the action. A legal move can still follow a false picture of the world.

  9. Before You Optimize a Prompt, Find Out What Can Improve

    Find measurable headroom, isolate which part of a task failed, and protect a held-out test before using reflective prompt search.

  10. Is Your Vision Model Reading the Image or Believing the Caption?

    Use controlled image and caption changes to distinguish visual grounding from a convincing answer built on text alone.

What to review

  • Purpose and usefulness. After the opening, can you explain the problem the article addresses and the decision it helps a reader make? Identify anything that feels too basic, unhelpful or unfinished.
  • Clarity. Mark unfamiliar terms, confusing sentences and missing steps. Can a reader new to local AI follow the worked example without guessing what a component does?
  • Academic reasoning. Does the research explain the mechanism rather than merely lend authority? Are equations, units and assumptions explained, and is their practical relevance clear?
  • Evidence and limits. Check important claims against the linked primary paper and cited table or section. Do model, task, sample size, prompt and evaluation conditions support the conclusion?
  • Examples and calculations. Follow the arithmetic, constraints and diagrams. Distinguish original teaching examples, hypothetical numbers, reported research and proposed tests. Flag any claim that sounds like an experiment the author actually ran.
  • Practical next step. Could you try the proposed diagnostic or use the explanation in a real local-AI decision? Flag missing inputs, inaccessible resources or instructions that overpromise.
  • Sundog's voice. Does she sound like a clear, capable engineer with a point of view? Mark vague slogans, awkward metaphors, repetitive endings or claims of experience unsupported by evidence.
  • Hero and presentation. Is Sundog recognizable across the collection? Does each scene show the article's idea with coherent future gear and a plausible setting? Check the article on a phone, including figures, table scrolling, source links and full-size art.
  • Collection value. Does this article have its own central idea? Note unnecessary overlap, missing connections or a topic that deserves a separate follow-up.

How to return useful feedback

For each article, give one verdict: ready, minor edits, or substantial revision. Quote a short phrase or name the section, describe the issue and suggest what would make it clearer or better supported. Include a source when correcting a factual claim. Keep must-fix issues separate from optional improvements.

A useful note looks like: “Article / section / short phrase → what is unclear or unsupported → suggested change or source.” A brief overall assessment of the collection is welcome.
