A Procedure Needs More Than a Clever Prompt

Compare putting a workflow in context, enforcing its transitions in code, and teaching behavior through an adapter. They preserve different kinds of control.

By Sundog

29 September 2026 · 6 min read · Engineering concepts

A community equipment library asks an assistant to help members borrow a device. The conversation sounds polished. The assistant checks availability, explains the deposit and ends with a friendly confirmation. It has also skipped the membership check that the procedure requires.

Teaching an assistant to sound familiar with a procedure is easier to observe than teaching it to follow every branch correctly. Where should the procedure live: in the prompt, in code that controls the route, or in a trained adapter? Recent papers disagree in useful ways because they preserve different things.

I would separate three requirements before choosing: understanding the whole procedure, tracking the current case, and enforcing actions that must not occur out of order. One storage location need not do all three jobs.

A small procedure makes the distinction concrete

This original lending example has four decisions. Check membership; check whether the requested device is available; confirm the deposit; issue the device. An unavailable device can lead to a waitlist. Missing membership information must lead to a question, rather than an invented answer.

Conversation quality and procedural success are different scores

RequirementEvidence of success
Helpful conversationThe member understands the options.

Valid actionIssuing is allowed only after membership, availability and deposit are established.

The prompt can carry the whole map. An application can carry the current node and permit only particular transitions. Training can make some behaviors more likely without repeatedly supplying the full map. These are different forms of control, not interchangeable implementations.

Whole-procedure context can outperform a particular router

In-Context Prompting Obsoletes Agent Orchestration for Procedural Tasks compares a full flowchart in the system prompt with a LangGraph design that supplies node-specific templates and adds decision calls. Across three simulated customer-service domains, its Claude Sonnet 4.5 in-context condition receives higher primary-judge task-success scores on a 1–5 scale. For insurance, judged failure, defined as a score of 3 or below, is 5.0% versus 17.0%, over 200 conversations per condition. [1]

But that condition uses more tokens, and the paper explicitly does not establish the result for smaller models or tool workflows with external state. Its title is broader than the tested comparison. The finding is a reason to question a router that hides useful global context, not permission to remove every enforcement layer. [1]

In our lending example, a model seeing only “confirm the deposit” may lose the reasons for requesting it or the conditions that should stop the loan. Supplying the whole procedure can preserve that relationship. An application can still refuse an invalid checkout.

Finishing the conversation does not prove an adapter learned the route

The companion study Procedural Knowledge Is Not Low-Rank trains LoRA adapters and full fine-tunes on related procedures. For Qwen2.5-3B travel conversations, LoRA completion is 95.5–99.0%, while primary-judge task-success scores on a 1–5 scale are 2.10–2.54 compared with 4.11 for full fine-tuning. Those judge scores are not a direct audit of every transition. [2]

The gaps vary by domain and judge; the Qwen3-8B Zoom task has a much smaller gap than insurance. The paper does not prove that every adapter fails to learn every procedure. It shows why termination and language-model loss are insufficient acceptance criteria for these workflows. [2]

For a lending assistant, a completed dialogue could conceal a skipped eligibility check. A path audit would ask whether the required checks occurred and whether each terminal action was justified. It supplies a kind of evidence a fluency score cannot.

What “low rank” limits, and what it does not tell you

A standard LoRA update writes a change to a weight matrix as ΔW = BA. If the original matrix has m × n entries and the adapter rank is r, the two factors have m × r and r × n entries. The update’s rank is at most r. This is the construction described in the original LoRA paper. [3]

For an illustrative 4,096 × 4,096 matrix, the full matrix has 16,777,216 entries. A rank-16 adapter has 131,072 entries across its two factors, about 0.78% as many. Rank 128 has 1,048,576, or 6.25%. These counts explain the attraction of adapters; they do not predict the accuracy of a particular procedure.

The procedural study also examines how much of a full fine-tune’s update can fit into its leading singular directions. The relevant ratio is:

retained update energy = sum(top r singular values squared)
                         / sum(all singular values squared)

Here “energy” means squared matrix magnitude, not electricity. The study reports mean retained fractions of 42.5–50.8% across the analyzed matrices for its procedure updates at rank 128. These are averages of matrix-level ratios, not the ratio for one concatenated whole-model update. They cannot be read as the same percentage of useful behavior, or proof that a procedure requires full fine-tuning. [2]

A model’s behavior depends on where an update acts as well as its magnitude. An instruction, a code guard and an adapter also intervene at different points. A low-rank count is useful for understanding a constraint, but it cannot replace the end-to-end workflow check.

Keep three objects separate

I would store the procedure as a versioned description, the case state as explicit data, and the action permissions in the application that executes them. This is an engineering proposal, not a combined result demonstrated by the papers.

For the lending assistant, case state might be membership=confirmed, stock=available, deposit=unknown. The assistant can explain the procedure naturally, but an issue-device action remains unavailable until the missing deposit decision is resolved. Code checks known requirements; the model interprets language and asks useful questions.

That division also makes changes easier to reason about. A deposit policy updated today belongs in a maintained rule or current procedure. It should not require hoping that a previously trained adapter has somehow changed its mind. An adapter may still be useful for stable communication habits or familiar task patterns.

Skill-to-LoRA offers a useful counterpoint. On a selected software-skill benchmark, it reports 65 solved tasks out of 210 with skill adapters, compared with 59 without a skill and 54 with the full skill text. The comparison is a single run on Qwen3.6-27B, without reported significance tests; it concerns software-skill documents, not a proof that adapters can enforce our lending flowchart. [4]

Try the inexpensive comparison before training

For a local installation, begin with two versions of the same small procedure. One provides the complete map in context. The other provides the current step plus a compact view of the global conditions. Use the same scenario cards, including an unavailable device, missing membership data and a member who changes the request midway through the conversation.

Inspect whether the final action is allowed, whether an essential check was skipped, and whether the user received an understandable explanation. Keep a state-tagged debugging run separate from the ordinary dialogue: requiring a model to print its current node can itself change how it behaves.

If the complete map helps, improve what the model can see before buying a training project. If it still skips mandatory conditions, enforce those conditions at execution. If a narrow, stable behavior remains worth learning, an adapter becomes a candidate with its own test, rather than a substitute for every other form of control.

[OPINION] A good procedure should survive a charming assistant. I want the conversational freedom where it helps the member and an explicit rule where the next action needs a guarantee. The useful design question is which part of that promise each component can actually keep.
