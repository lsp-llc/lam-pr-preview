More Agents Can Make the Answer Worse

Distinguish independent attempts, checking, and debate. Shared mistakes and persuasion can consume a budget without adding reliable evidence.

By Sundog

29 September 2026 · 6 min read · Agent engineering

You have a local model and a difficult question. Running three copies seems like an inexpensive way to add intelligence: let them disagree, argue, and reach a better answer. But three conversations can also give the same mistake three chances to sound persuasive.

The important distinction is between collecting independent attempts and letting those attempts influence one another. A vote, a debate, a checker and a division of labor are different systems. Recent multi-agent research shows why extra discussion can lose an advantage that independent answers already had.

Three answers can help before anybody starts talking

Suppose each of three independent attempts is correct with probability p, and the result is accepted when at least two are correct. In a simplified binary setting, majority accuracy is:

P(majority correct) = 3p²(1 − p) + p³
                    = 3p² − 2p³

At p = 0.7, the result is 0.784, or 78.4%. At p = 0.4, it is 35.2%. This original calculation assumes independent, identically distributed outcomes and a checkable binary answer. It explains why copying a model is not automatically an improvement even under generous assumptions.

Real model errors can be correlated: the copies share training, a prompt, an interpretation and often the same misleading evidence. If all three always make the same decision, the majority has exactly the accuracy of one. For open-ended answers, disagreement between wrong answers also complicates what “majority” means. A count of matching sentences is not a correctness test.

Discussion can undo a useful independent vote

Talk Isn’t Always Cheap compares votes over initial answers with votes after two debate rounds. For three Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct copies on GSM8K, Table 1 reports 76.4% for a single agent, 87.6% for the initial majority, and 84.2% after debate. The majority and debate means have standard errors of 1.5 and 2.0 points; no significance test is reported. The study uses 100 sampled questions per task and five seeds, with hosted open-model endpoints. [1]

The debate result still beats the single agent in that comparison, while losing 3.4 percentage points from the initial vote. Other cells improve, and some small differences sit within reported uncertainty. This is evidence to examine the effect of discussion, not a rule that debate always hurts. [1]

Once agents see each other’s answers, their later outputs are no longer independent attempts. A correct dissenter can be persuaded to join a wrong majority. The paper examines these answer changes; agreement at the end can conceal a loss of correct information earlier in the process. [1]

For a local system, keeping the first answers lets you ask a revealing question: did the discussion create a correct answer, preserve one, or talk the group out of it? You cannot answer that by scoring only the final consensus.

The best member, the average member and an oracle are different baselines

Multi-Agent Teams Hold Experts Back studies teams with differing expertise. It distinguishes a best individual from an oracle that succeeds whenever any member had the right answer. An oracle can inspect correctness after the fact; a working team has to decide which member to trust without that privilege. [2]

That distinction changes the headline. The paper’s 6.3–41.1% gaps on its machine-learning benchmarks are relative to the at-least-one-correct oracle, not simply losses against the best individual. When the expert is revealed, teams exceed the best individual on four of five benchmarks. That identity is selected per problem using answer correctness, so this is an oracle-informed condition rather than a deployable expert-routing method. [2]

For deployment, I would want the attainable comparator: one capable model with the same work budget, or independent candidates followed by a selection method the application can actually execute. “Somewhere in the team was the answer” is useful for diagnosing a selector. It does not describe a finished system.

Use parallelism where the work can actually split

Towards a Science of Scaling Agent Systems compares several coordination architectures on tool-using tasks. Its results differ sharply between a decomposable finance task and sequential planning: some multi-agent designs help the former and hurt the latter. The tested models are hosted frontier models, so the magnitudes should not be transplanted onto a local 8B setup. [3]

The transferable question is structural. Can two workers produce independently checkable pieces, or does each next action depend on the state left by the last one? Searching two separate document collections can divide cleanly. Letting two agents simultaneously edit the same configuration can create conflicting assumptions, even if each edit looks sensible in isolation.

A checker also needs a different job from the solver. “Find an unsupported source, an invalid step or a violated constraint” gives it evidence to inspect. “Debate until you agree” supplies a social stopping condition. Neither guarantees correctness, but only one specifies the defect the extra work is supposed to catch.

Compare systems under the same work allowance

Here is an original small comparison you can design around your own local tasks. Give the system a total generation allowance, then compare one solver, three independent attempts with a defined selector, and a solver followed by a targeted check. Add debate only if you want to measure its effect separately.

For example, a 3,000-token allowance could fund one 3,000-token attempt, three 800-token attempts plus a 600-token selector, or a 1,500-token solver, an 800-token checker and a 700-token revision. Those allocations are illustrative choices, not equal-cost benchmarks: prompt processing, tool calls and repeated model loading can change latency. They do prevent giving one architecture unlimited extra output while calling the comparison fair.

Choose tasks with answers or acceptance rules you can check. Keep the initial outputs. If a revision changes a correct answer to a wrong one, identify what evidence caused the change. If the selector never finds the correct candidate, increasing the number of candidates may add cost without solving the bottleneck.

On a single device, parallel conversations may share the same compute resources. More simultaneous requests do not imply each completes as quickly as a single request. Measure complete-job latency and useful results along with generated tokens.

A team should add a capability you can name

A source finder can bring evidence the solver did not have. A deterministic checker can reject a configuration that violates a rule. A second specialist can inspect a domain the first model handles poorly. Those are identifiable contributions.

Three copies can still be useful, particularly when sampling produces diverse candidate solutions and a reliable check can choose among them. The point is to make that diversity and selection visible rather than assuming a conversation will manufacture expertise.

[OPINION] I would pay for another agent when I can say what it will contribute and how I will notice if it fails. If its job is simply to agree more elaborately, I would keep the independent answers and spend the remaining budget checking them.
