Is Your Vision Model Reading the Image or Believing the Caption?

Use controlled image and caption changes to distinguish visual grounding from a convincing answer built on text alone.

By Sundog

29 September 2026 · 6 min read · Start here

You show a vision model a chart and write, “The orange bar is taller.” It agrees and explains why. That explanation does not tell you whether it read the chart, followed your sentence, or guessed from a familiar pattern.

A model can answer questions about images while making poor use of a particular image. The useful test is to change the evidence in a controlled way. Keep the question fixed, change the picture, remove it, or supply a caption that conflicts with it. Then inspect which changes affect correctness.

Research on visual representations and nonlocal visual reasoning gives this test a scientific motivation. The two studies examine different bottlenecks: information available inside a vision system, and the ability to follow relationships across an image. Neither proves that your current local vision model ignores pictures. They help you ask a better question about it. [1] [2]

The image and its description are different inputs

A typical vision-language model has a vision encoder that converts pixels into features, a connector that presents those features to the language model, and a language model that generates the answer. Having useful visual features does not guarantee that the final answer uses them correctly.

Hidden in plain sight compares visual readouts with complete VLM answers and a blank-image control. Its main experiments use Vicuna-based 7B systems with different vision encoders. For the DINOv2-backed system on low-level matching, Table 1 reports accuracy of 0.819 for the visual readout, 0.170 for the full VLM, and 0.251 for the VLM with a blank image. [1]

A readout is a task-specific way to use the encoder’s representations; it is not the ordinary chat response. The gap shows why encoder capability and answer capability must be distinguished. The full model’s lower printed score with the image does not by itself show that it ignored the image. It can also be making image-dependent mistakes. The table does not supply item counts or error bars that would let us establish the uncertainty of that particular difference. [1]

The paper’s broader analyses compare answer distributions and probe representations through later layers. They support its diagnosis of a bottleneck in using visual features for those systems. This is stronger evidence than one wrong chat answer, but it remains evidence from the tested architectures and tasks. A newer checkpoint needs its own evaluation.

Recognizing an object is not tracing a relationship

VLMs have Tunnel Vision builds tests for object matching, following a chain of visual references and tracing circuit connections. These tasks require combining information from different parts of an image. Seeing each local shape is insufficient if the model connects them incorrectly. [2]

In a decomposed three-step scavenger-hunt test, its Table 1 reports 90.67% accuracy for o4-mini, while Qwen2.5-VL-7B scores 14.67%, across 75 trials. This is the decomposed protocol, not the benchmark’s end-to-end score. The prompt, examples and staged assistance change what is being measured. It is not a leaderboard for ordinary captioning. [2]

The study gives us a reason to separate easy visual reading from relationship-heavy work. A model that correctly names colors can still lose a wire at a crossing. A model that reads a label can still attach it to the wrong object. For a local assistant interpreting screenshots or diagrams, those associations may be the entire job.

Start with a chart whose answer you control

The following is an original teaching example, not a benchmark result. Both charts have the same two bars and colors; their heights swap. The question is always: which bar is taller, blue or orange? Chart A’s answer is blue. Chart B’s is orange.

For the first condition, supply one chart with a neutral instruction: answer from the image. For another, supply the same image with “The orange bar is taller” presented as an accompanying claim, and explicitly ask the model to check that claim against the image. That claim conflicts with A and agrees with B. Reverse the claim in another pair so each color appears equally often as the suggested answer.

A model that always says orange can look successful on B without reading anything. Balance correct answers and caption claims. Change positions as well as heights across further examples, so a rule such as “choose the left bar” cannot pass. Use fresh images and reset the conversation between conditions; an earlier answer can contaminate a later check.

A blank-image control asks whether the pixels helped

Repeat the neutral condition with a blank image of the same dimensions. Keep the question and any text identical. The model may guess or say it cannot determine the answer. Record those behaviors separately. Do not treat a reasonable abstention on a blank image as a failure to use vision.

Let Areal be accuracy with the real images and Ablank accuracy under the blank condition. Their difference, Δ = Areal − Ablank, estimates how much the images helped on that set. It does not measure whether the model’s internal representation contains the needed information.

For an explicitly hypothetical set of 40 balanced questions, 32 correct real-image answers and 21 correct blank-image answers give Δ = 11/40 = 27.5 percentage points. These totals do not reveal which individual answers changed. Retain paired outcomes and use question-level uncertainty; do not infer a precise general effect from two small totals.

If real-image accuracy is low but answers change when pixels change, the model may be visually sensitive yet unreliable. If accuracy and answers stay similar under blanking, language priors, answer-position bias or leaked clues may explain the apparent performance. If both conditions score highly, inspect the task text for an answer that does not require the image.

Test the visual operation your work requires

The two-bar task is a setup check. Passing it does not establish that a model can trace a network diagram or understand a scientific plate. Add examples that represent your job: identifying which panel owns a warning, following a connector between two components, or matching a legend to a plotted series.

Change one relationship at a time where possible. Start with uncrossed connectors, then add crossings while preserving the correct endpoint. Keep image resolution and the application’s resizing behavior in your record. A downsampled wire or cropped label can turn a reasoning comparison into a different perception task.

Allow a reasoning response if that is how you intend to use the model, and score the final answer separately from the explanation. If you also test a direct-answer prompt, identify it as a separate condition. Forcing a short answer changes the protocol; it should not silently be treated as a reproduction of research that allowed step-by-step tracing.

This article proposes a local diagnostic. I have not measured its result on a current checkpoint. A modern model that handles the image swaps, blank controls and conflicting claims correctly is a useful positive result for that model and workload. The purpose is to find what you can depend on, not to arrange a failure.

[OPINION] I want an image-reading assistant that can disagree with an attractive caption when the pixels say otherwise. A convincing description is pleasant; a controlled change in the picture that produces the right change in the answer gives me a firmer reason to trust it.
