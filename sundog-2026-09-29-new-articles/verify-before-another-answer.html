Check the Candidate Before You Ask for Another Answer

Turn a plausible answer into explicit constraints, understand the limits of self-checking, and choose where an independent verifier earns its cost.

By Sundog

29 September 2026 · 6 min read · Practical model use

A local model gives you a neat answer. You ask again, and it gives you a different neat answer. Now you have two candidates and the same problem: which one survives contact with the question?

I would spend the next step on a check. That can mean substituting a number into an equation, running a test, or inspecting a proposed schedule against its constraints. It can also mean asking the model to check a candidate before solving. Those are different levels of evidence, and the difference matters when you are paying for every extra token with your own hardware.

Two research lines make an interesting pair. Verification-first prompting reports useful gains on several reasoning benchmarks. Logic-grid research shows that producing more candidates can help much less than selecting them with a reliable checker. Together they suggest a practical question: is your bottleneck finding a candidate, or recognizing a good one? [1] [2]

A candidate gives the checker something concrete to reject

Here is an original small puzzle. Three technicians—Ada, Bo and Cy—have one appointment each, at 09:00, 10:00 and 11:00. Each brings a different kit: amber, blue or green. Ada is not first. Bo brings blue. The green kit arrives at 11:00. Cy arrives before Ada.

Suppose a model proposes this schedule:

TimeTechnicianKit
09:00AdaAmber
10:00BoBlue
11:00CyGreen

It uses every name, time and kit exactly once. It satisfies the blue and green clues. It still fails: Ada is first, and Cy comes after her. Checking only whether the output is a tidy table would miss both errors.

Nor does rejecting this candidate give you one unique answer. These clues permit two schedules. Ada must be third with green: putting her second would leave Bo last, where his blue kit would contradict the green-kit clue. Cy and Bo can occupy the first two slots in either order, with amber and blue respectively. A checker needs to accept both valid schedules, rather than compare the text with one preferred answer.

That is the first useful distinction: format validity, constraint satisfaction and uniqueness are separate properties. For this toy puzzle, a few explicit checks can settle the first two, and enumeration can settle the third. A fluent explanation settles none of them by itself.

What verification-first prompting actually found

Asking LLMs to Verify First is Almost Free Lunch supplies a candidate—even the trivial number 1 for mathematical questions—and asks the model to examine it before finding an answer. The point is to make a violated condition visible early in the response. The authors propose that this directs subsequent reasoning toward relevant constraints; that explanation is a mechanism they argue for, rather than one they directly establish. [1]

In its Table 3, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct improves from 84.15% to 88.60% on GSM8K and from 45.60% to 52.60% on MATH500. These are gains of 4.45 and 7.00 percentage points in the reported protocol. Across the tested models, average output length rises from 365.6 to 533.6 tokens on GSM8K—about 46%—and from 808.3 to 1,109.6 on MATH500—about 37%. The improvement has a cost, even when no second model is involved. [1]

The tested open models are instruct releases from Qwen2.5 and Llama 3, with different sizes evaluated on different benchmark subsets. The paper does not test Qwen3, an open-weight thinking model, or our scheduling puzzle. It also does not report seeds or confidence intervals. Its aggregate gains are a reason to investigate this prompt structure, not a promised uplift for your current download. [1]

The mathematical protocol includes a supplied response opening, not just a user instruction. If you paste “check this candidate first” into an ordinary chat, you are trying a verification-first-style prompt. You are not necessarily reproducing that protocol. This is especially relevant in an app that controls the model’s template and does not let you prefill its response.

Checking with the same model has a ceiling

A model can miss a constraint while generating and miss it again while checking. It can also rationalize the candidate you supplied. The verification-first paper acknowledges cases in which ordinary reasoning was right and verification-first was wrong, particularly for smaller models. Asking for a check adds an instruction and a reasoning burden; it does not add an independent source of truth. [1]

ZebraLogic makes that limitation visible on a different task. It contains 1,000 logic-grid puzzles with explicitly checkable solutions. For GPT-4o, its baseline puzzle accuracy is 31.7%. One self-verification round reaches 33.0%; a second reaches 32.1%. By contrast, generating 128 candidates and using an oracle that already knows correctness reaches 69.1%. Majority voting over those 128 candidates reaches 37.6%. [2]

The oracle result says correct candidates were often somewhere in the sample. It does not supply a deployable selector. A vote can prefer the repeated wrong answer, and a learned judge can have its own blind spots. The small self-verification gain also does not refute the first paper: the tasks, models and prompt protocols differ. It tells us to test the proposed transfer instead of treating “verification” as one universally effective operation.

The math explains why a larger search can disappoint

With n appointment slots and two independently assigned attributes—technician and kit—the unconstrained assignment space has (n!)² possibilities. Our three-slot example starts with 36. Five slots start with 14,400. Six start with 518,400. Clues prune that space, so these counts are not measured model effort or a solver’s required number of steps. They show why adding a few entities can radically change the problem. [2]

Once a full candidate exists, each explicit clue may be cheap to inspect. A rule such as “Cy before Ada” becomes one comparison between two positions. This is why a programmatic checker can be valuable even when a generator is unreliable. The whole answer must satisfy all clues; checking one convenient clue is insufficient.

A different paper, Scaling Test-Time Compute Without Verification or RL is Suboptimal, develops theory about training with verifiers or rewards versus verifier-free training. Its conclusions depend on assumptions about the base model’s candidate distribution. It does not prove that a self-checking chat prompt beats ordinary reasoning, and the exercise here does not test its theorem. The common engineering concern is the value of a usable correctness signal; the interventions are different. [3]

Try checking ability separately from solving ability

You can explore this on a local instruct model without building an agent. Use fresh puzzles whose constraints you can evaluate yourself. In separate chats, ask the model to solve a puzzle, assess a valid candidate, and assess a candidate with one deliberately introduced violation. Keep the clue set identical and do not label which candidate is wrong.

For a fourth condition, give the wrong candidate and ask it to identify violated clues before proposing a replacement. Record whether it found the planted violation and whether its replacement satisfies every clue. A better explanation and a better final answer are different outcomes.

This is a proposed diagnostic, not a result I have measured. Use several puzzle structures and sizes; the tiny example above mainly teaches the grading rule. If you compare prompt conditions, preserve the checkpoint, quantization, template and generation settings, and count output tokens and truncations. Randomize condition order. A candidate that reveals extra information can make the task easier for reasons beyond the checking instruction.

[OPINION] I like a model that can tell me exactly which condition an answer violates. I trust that judgment further when the condition is checked by code or direct observation. For a job with a clear validator, my first extra component would be that validator. A second stream of plausible answers can wait until I know how to choose among them.
