A Better Math Score Doesn’t Promise a Better Assistant

Separate specialization, transfer, and sample coverage before choosing a reasoning checkpoint for mixed everyday work.

By Sundog

29 September 2026 · 5 min read · Model selection

A checkpoint posts an impressive math score. You download it as your new everyday assistant, then discover it adds long reasoning markers to simple answers, handles a required format awkwardly, or changes how it responds to ordinary questions.

A specialist result tells you something real about the measured specialty. It does not tell you which other behaviors were preserved. Research on reasoning post-training makes that distinction sharper: a model can improve its first attempt on one task while changing transfer, response format, or the range of solutions it produces under a larger sampling budget.

The question for a local installation is whether those changes fit your mixture of work. A better specialist can be worth keeping beside a general assistant without replacing it.

Accuracy is a vector before it becomes a headline

Suppose your workload contains mathematical questions, short document answers and strict-format extraction. Collapsing all three into one mean can hide a gain in the first and a loss in the last. The weight you put on each should reflect your job, not the checkpoint’s most flattering benchmark.

A paper on transfer from math reasoning includes a controlled Qwen3-8B-Base ablation. Its Table 4 reports the following averages across its benchmark groups. “On-policy” here refers to using responses sampled from the evolving model during training; “off-policy” uses a fixed source of training responses. The first fine-tune gains more on math while losing seven points in the non-reasoning average. The second makes a different tradeoff. These are one study’s aggregate scores, without reported seed variation; they do not establish a universal advantage for one training label. The study also discusses reasoning-format drift, which can affect how non-reasoning outputs are scored. [1]

For selection, a format failure can still be a real operational failure. If an application requires valid JSON and the model adds an explanation, the job is unfinished. But that is a different diagnosis from saying the model no longer knows the requested fact.

A stronger first attempt can coexist with narrower sampled coverage

Does Reinforcement Learning Really Incentivize Reasoning Capacity in LLMs Beyond the Base Model? compares pass@1 with success under many samples. In several tested settings, the reinforcement-learned model improves at small sample budgets while the base model catches up or surpasses it at larger budgets. The paper also reports settings that do not fit a simple “RL always narrows capability” rule. [2]

Pass@k means that at least one of k sampled candidates is correct. It is an oracle-style coverage measure unless you also have a way to recognize and select that correct candidate. A chat user usually receives one answer, not a labeled bucket of every possible solution.

For a toy problem with independent samples and per-sample correctness probability p:

pass@k = 1 − (1 − p)^k

The expression is useful for intuition. Published empirical pass@k estimation and real sampled outputs have additional details; repeating a deterministic response does not create independent opportunities.

Two problems show why the ranking can reverse

This original example uses two equally weighted problems. A general model has correctness probabilities 0.60 and 0.10; a specialist has 0.90 and 0.01. These are assumed values, not observations about any checkpoint.

General0.600.1035.0%78.4%
Specialist0.900.0145.5%53.9%

For the general model on Problem B, eight chances yield 1 − 0.9^8 ≈ 56.95%. For the specialist, they yield 1 − 0.99^8 ≈ 7.73%. Averaging the two problems’ probabilities gives the table. You must calculate per problem before averaging; using one overall success rate loses the unevenness that drives this example.

The specialist wins at one attempt by becoming very dependable on A. At eight attempts, the general model’s greater chance on B matters more. Neither ranking is “the true model quality” without a work budget and selection method.

This toy model does not prove that a training method caused such a change. It shows what the two measurements ask. The research’s actual sampled coverage results belong to its checkpoints, tasks and budgets. [2]

Training changes how an existing repertoire is used

Echo Chamber studies small models trained on fully specified math-data mixtures and then post-trained with reinforcement learning. It finds that training can concentrate output on one of the available response styles, often the more accurate one. The chosen style is not necessarily the one that was most frequent before post-training. [3]

That is an explanatory contrast, not evidence about every community reasoning merge. The study’s controlled 150M and 1B models let the authors inspect data mixtures that are not known for many downloadable assistants. A comparison between unrelated checkpoint releases cannot recover that causal control. [3]

For the user, the visible questions remain practical: does the model answer directly when needed, follow the output contract, solve the specialist task, and retain useful behavior elsewhere? Its training description helps interpret those results; it cannot stand in for them.

Select for the jobs you will actually send

Before replacing your general assistant, try representative examples from both the specialty and the routine work. Score strict formatting separately from factual or mathematical correctness. Keep prompt templates compatible with each checkpoint, and disclose those differences if you compare results.

If you want to inspect candidate coverage, choose tasks with an independent checker and a manageable sample budget. A pass@8 comparison requires at least eight actual samples for each item and condition. Count truncations; a short cap that cuts off the specialist’s answer can confound the result.

This is a proposed selection exercise, not controlled retraining. Changing checkpoint families, teachers, precision and templates changes several variables together. It may tell you which release fits your machine and job, but not which training ingredient caused the difference.

[OPINION] I would keep a specialist that earns its place on difficult work and retain the ordinary assistant when it handles ordinary work better. Disk space permits choices that a leaderboard’s single ranking hides. The useful upgrade is a routing decision you understand, not a ceremony in which the newest checkpoint inherits every job.
