Before You Optimize a Prompt, Find Out What Can Improve

Find measurable headroom, isolate which part of a task failed, and protect a held-out test before using reflective prompt search.

By Sundog

29 September 2026 · 6 min read · Engineering concepts

You revise a prompt, rerun a handful of questions and get a better score. That is encouraging. It becomes less convincing when the next handful reverses the result.

Prompt optimization searches over instructions while leaving the model’s weights fixed. It can discover a useful output rule or a better sequence of tool calls. It can also discover wording that happens to score well on the examples you keep showing it. Before turning your local machine into a prompt-search engine, identify the failure you want to repair and reserve examples that the search never gets to inspect.

One recent study reports frequent failures to improve over a plain prompt; another, GEPA, finds substantial improvements on some tasks with an open-weight 8B model. These are compatible findings. They use different models, tasks, feedback and data budgets. The interesting engineering question is where useful headroom exists in your system. [1] [2]

Find out which part of the answer failed

Imagine an original local help-desk task: extract a device identifier, a symptom and a severity from a short ticket, then return an exact JSON object. Your application can fail because the response is invalid JSON, because a required key is absent, or because the extracted content is wrong. Those failures call for different repairs.

Suppose a hypothetical set of 100 tickets contains 80 answers with valid structure, 75 with correct content, and 65 satisfying both. The application’s end-to-end success is 65%. That is an intersection measured item by item; multiplying 0.80 by 0.75 would assume independence and give 60%, which does not describe these hypothetical observations.

A format instruction may help some of the 10 content-correct answers that failed structure. It cannot be credited with fixing content merely because more responses now parse. Conversely, a better extractor may still be unusable if it violates the output contract. Score structure, content and combined success separately.

Run a few targeted probes on development examples. Show the schema explicitly. Include one representative example. Ask for a field without requiring the final format. Check whether supplying missing context fixes the content error. These probes help distinguish instruction trouble from unavailable information or a model capability limit. They are proposed diagnostics, not a proven predictor of optimization gains.

The “coin flip” result has a specific scope

Prompt Optimization Is a Coin Flip compares six optimizers across four tasks, with three repeats on Claude Haiku 4.5. It reports that 49% of those 72 runs score below the untouched zero-shot prompt. This is a result for those conditions, not a probability that every prompt revision has a 49% chance of failure. No open-weight executor is tested. [1]

One task, HelpSteer2, is the clear positive case: all six methods improve over the Haiku baseline, with the best reported mean score rising from 68.0 to 74.8 on the study’s 0–100 judge scale. The authors propose that a structured-output requirement offers something the model can do but does not do by default. They explicitly identify this explanation as a hypothesis from one positive task, unvalidated on held-out tasks. [1]

Changing the executor to Amazon Nova Lite changes the pattern. On HelpSteer2, only one of six methods beats its zero-shot baseline. A successful optimization strategy is therefore tied to the model and task together. The result does not establish that formatting is the only source of prompt headroom, or that a short probe can certify a task as unoptimizable.

GEPA learns from the trace, not just the score

GEPA asks a reflection model to inspect runs and textual feedback, propose changed instructions and evaluate them. It retains candidates with complementary strengths across validation examples, rather than keeping only whichever prompt currently has the best average. The executor’s weights remain unchanged. [2]

For Qwen3-8B, the paper’s Table 1 reports HotpotQA improving from 42.33 to 62.33 and HoVer from 35.33 to 52.33. IFBench moves much less, from 36.90 to 38.61. The six-task aggregate rises from 45.23 to 54.85, but that average hides markedly different task gains. GRPO’s reported AIME-2025 score exceeds GEPA’s. [2]

These are comparisons against the study’s seed prompts, not against every careful human-written prompt. The reported scores are point estimates without seed variance or confidence intervals. Its non-math tasks also use substantially more labeled development data than the 20 training examples in the coin-flip study: GEPA has training and validation sets with hundreds of examples combined. Neither paper tests whether the other paper’s cheap headroom probe predicts GEPA’s gains. [1] [2]

This makes GEPA an interesting method to investigate when you have useful traces and grading feedback. It does not make optimization free. The budget includes executor runs, reflection calls and validation. “Uses an open-weight executor” is also different from “every component runs locally”; inspect the reflection model and scoring setup before planning a fully local workflow.

A small score change may be one question

The coin-flip paper proposes a two-point headroom threshold under its judge-based scoring setup. On a different test with 20 binary-scored questions, one changed answer is already five percentage points. Borrowing the threshold without borrowing the metric gives it no useful protection against noise. [1]

Paired results are more informative than two totals. For each question, let D be +1 if the new prompt fixes a baseline failure, −1 if it breaks a baseline success, and 0 if correctness is unchanged. The mean d is the gain. Let q be the fraction of questions on which the two conditions differ in correctness. Then a plug-in standard error is approximately:

SE(d) ≈ √((q − d²) / N).

The equation follows because D² is 1 exactly on disagreements, so its mean is q; variance is the mean square minus the squared mean. It is an approximation for independent test items, not a substitute for the right analysis when items repeat, share templates or come from the same document.

For an illustrative 100 questions with 13 fixes and 8 newly broken answers, d = 0.05 and q = 0.21. SE is about 0.0456. A rough normal 95% interval is 0.05 ± 0.089: about −3.9 to +13.9 percentage points. A five-point gain on that hypothetical test does not settle the question. The 21% disagreement matters; changing it changes the uncertainty.

The unit is the independent question, not each of five samples from the same question. Repeated generation can measure sampling variability, but it does not turn 20 distinct problems into 100 independently drawn problems.

Separate the search from the verdict

Use development examples to diagnose failures and revise instructions. Use validation examples to choose a candidate. Then freeze the choice and evaluate it on a separate test set, with the baseline alongside it. If you keep inspecting that test and selecting new prompts from its failures, it has become another development set.

This separation matters even in manual search. Trying 20 variants gives you 20 opportunities to pick a lucky score. The chosen best score has been selected for looking good; it needs fresh evidence. A small development pilot can reveal a repair worth pursuing, but a flat pilot can also be inconclusive rather than proof of no headroom.

For a local comparison, retain the exact prompt, checkpoint, quantization, template and generation settings. Log both correctness flips and costs: input tokens, output tokens, latency and the search’s total calls. A longer winning instruction can increase the cost of every future request. A worthwhile improvement is one that survives fresh examples and earns that continuing cost.

[OPINION] I would start with a failure I can name: a missed field, an unsupported claim, a tool called in the wrong order. Give the search evidence about that failure. A prompt found this way has a reason to exist. I want the final score to tell me whether the reason held up beyond the examples that taught it.
