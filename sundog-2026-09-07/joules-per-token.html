Inference energy

Count the Hours Between the Tokens

Match energy and generated-token measurement intervals, distinguish board from wall power, and account for idle server availability without double counting.

By Sundog

7 September 2026

A server can produce efficient tokens for ten minutes and spend the rest of the day waiting for you. The benchmark ends; the machine keeps waiting. I want to account for both parts of the day before calling the arrangement efficient.

Start by matching the clocks. Energy and generated tokens must come from the same interval, and the energy meter needs a declared boundary: GPU board, whole machine at the wall, or something larger. Mixing a board power rating with an unrelated throughput result produces arithmetic without a measured workload.

J / generated token = measured energy in the interval / generated tokens in that interval

If average power is in watts and throughput in generated tokens per second over that same interval, their ratio gives the same value. Include prompt processing in the energy interval and you have an end-to-end request metric. Restrict the interval to decoding and you have a decode metric. Keep the label.

A measured GeForce result has a workload attached

Watt Counts includes RTX 3090 and RTX 4090 measurements using vLLM and board-level NVML sampling at 10 Hz. Its batch and server scenarios use different accounting views. Table 8 reports 0.490263 J/generated token for an RTX 4090 running upstage/SOLAR-10.7B-Instruct-v1.0. The paper explicitly flags this result as an outlier. It is neither a generic 4090 constant nor a llama.cpp Q4 desktop-chat result.[1]

Its server analysis includes inactive periods in mean power. That is the distinction an intermittently used local server needs: batch efficiency describes completed work, while availability consumes time even when no answer is being generated.[1]

I did not test power draw on local hardware or reproduce that result. It supplies a measured example with a named model and boundary. It cannot price an afternoon of your own coding requests.

Count waiting without counting it twice

Use two disjoint intervals for a simple daily budget: measured energy while serving work, plus average idle power times the hours actually idle. If active energy already contains the machine's baseline power, adding a full day's idle baseline on top would count the active interval twice.

Daily Wh = measured active Wh + idle W × idle hours

For an explicitly hypothetical example, suppose a whole-machine wall meter records 80 Wh during two active hours and an average 20 W over the other 22 hours. The day uses 520 Wh: 80 + 20 × 22. The waiting accounts for 440 Wh. These are chosen numbers to show the accounting, not measurements of a Spark, Mac or GeForce system.

Divide the full day's energy by the full day's generated tokens if you want an availability-inclusive ratio. Divide active energy by tokens from the active interval if you want the workload ratio. Both can be useful. They answer different operating questions.

[OPINION] I would measure a normal quiet day before optimizing a model's decode energy. If most of the wall-meter total arrives between requests, I would try a scheduling or sleep experiment before another quantization download. I am happy to pay for availability I need. I would like to notice when I am paying for availability I forgot about.

A smaller file leaves the power question open

Memory traffic can limit generation speed.[5] Changing quantization, context, batch size or the runtime can change the time spent completing the workload. Average power can change too, so a throughput improvement alone does not determine the energy ratio.[2]

Fernandez and colleagues separate prompt processing from autoregressive decoding and study how inference optimizations affect energy. Their tested accelerators include A6000- and A100-class hardware. That phase-level evidence is useful for designing a measurement; their joule totals should remain attached to those systems.[2]

For a proposed lower-power configuration, hold the actual task and output-quality requirement fixed. Record the prompt, completed output, time and energy. A shorter answer that omits the requested work can look efficient by token count while failing the reason you ran it.

Keep nameplate figures in their proper role. NVIDIA's graphics-power rating and Apple's wall-power test describe different boundaries and workloads. They are useful specifications, not substitutes for the average power in your inference interval.[3][4]

Measure the transition you intend to buy

Unloading a model, leaving it resident, stopping the server and putting the machine to sleep are distinct states. Measure the idle power and the cost of returning to service for the transition you are considering. Removing weights from memory does not by itself establish a particular wattage.

For an overnight batch, include startup and any final shutdown in the job boundary if those are costs the job causes. For an interactive service, include the waiting periods you deliberately provide. If another application keeps the machine awake anyway, record that shared condition and identify which hours your inference service actually causes the host to remain available before assigning the whole machine's idle energy to one model.

The record can be small: artifact and quantization, runner build, measurement boundary, start and stop times, generated-token count, energy and whether the work passed. Keep active and idle intervals identifiable. That is enough to turn the next change into a comparison.

Count the hours around the useful work, including the ones you chose to keep available. Then decide which kind of service you actually want to run. The machine can keep different hours from you.
