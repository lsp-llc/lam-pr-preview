Keep Qwen3.8-27B while GLM-5.3 wants 223 GB

By Sundog. Pulse desk.

31 August 2026 · Revised 25 September 2026

GLM-5.3 is now open-weight, and even its smallest version needs 223 GB of memory. Here is the arithmetic that keeps Qwen3.8-27B on a 24 GB card, what its reasoning setting costs, and what each license allows.

GLM-5.3's weights went public on 28 August, and even its smallest version needs 223 GB of memory. [1] [2] Qwen3.8-27B needs 16 to 19 GB at 4 bits, and with a 4-bit cache it can hold a 256K-token conversation on one 24 GB graphics card for text work. [3] [4] If that card is your machine, the call has not changed: keep the 27B. The arithmetic behind these numbers applies to every large release.

The call for 31 August

  • Qwen3.8-27BKeep
    A 4-bit version needs 16 to 19 GB, so one 24 GB card runs it with room for the conversation. Apache-2.0. Pulse grade B− (71).
  • Qwen3.8-Flash-Next75 GB or more

  • GLM-5.3223 GB or more
    Needs 223 GB of memory even as a "1-bit" file. Z.AI's own license. No Pulse grade yet.

Count in 24 GB cards

A model file is mostly its weights, and its size is roughly the number of parameters times the bits stored for each, divided by eight to get bytes. Qwen3.8-27B stores 27.78 billion parameters, a small vision encoder and draft head included. [8] At the 16 bits it ships in, that is about 56 GB. A 4-bit file averages more than 4 bits per weight, because each block of weights also stores scales and the quantizer keeps some tensors at higher precision. [9] For this model, 4-bit files run from about 15 GB to 19 GB depending on who made them; the Q4_K_M that Quesma tested was 17.0 GB. [10] [11] Unsloth's guide asks for 16 to 19 GB of memory for its 4-bit versions, which leaves room on a 24 GB card for the conversation. [3]

GLM-5.3 is built differently. It is a mixture-of-experts model: in 75 of its 78 layers, a router sends each token through 8 of 256 specialist blocks, called experts, plus one shared expert. [1] Unsloth lists it as 744 billion parameters with 40 billion active per token, the figures Z.AI published for GLM-5, which has the same dimensions. [2] The Hugging Face checkpoint stores 753.3 billion, because it also carries a 10-billion-parameter layer that drafts upcoming tokens to speed up generation. [1] The active count sets how much work each token takes. The total sets how much memory you need: any expert can be chosen for the next token, so all of them must be loaded. The bandwidth guide works through what that means for speed.

At its smallest, GLM-5.3 needs the memory of almost nine such cards. The smallest setup Unsloth describes as working well is its 2-bit UD-IQ2_M file, 238.6 GB, on a machine with 256 GB of memory, such as two linked DGX Sparks or a Mac Studio. [2]

A "1-bit" file that averages 2.3 bits

Unsloth's smallest GLM-5.3 file, UD-IQ1_S, is labeled 1-bit and weighs 216.7 GB. [13] Divide by the parameter count and the average comes out much higher:

216,715,365,893 bytes × 8
  ÷ 753,329,940,480 parameters
  = 2.30 bits per weight

The file's own headers show where the extra bits went. Only 45 percent of the weights use IQ1_S, llama.cpp's 1.56-bit format. About 30 percent use a 3-bit format, 19 percent a 2-bit format, and the last 6 percent a mix of other formats, up to full 32-bit precision. [13] [9]

The mix is deliberate. When Unsloth introduced the method on DeepSeek-R1, it kept the small parts that every token passes through (attention, the shared expert, the first three dense layers, the embeddings) at 4 to 6 bits, left the router and normalization layers at full 32-bit precision, and pushed most of the routed experts, about 88 percent of that model, down to about 1.58 bits. [14] Squeezing everything evenly broke the model. The uniform version repeated itself endlessly, and on Unsloth's Flappy Bird coding test a uniform file of about the same size scored 0 out of 10 where the dynamic one scored 6.92. [14]

The same division checks any file. GLM-5.3's 8-bit file is 801.4 GB, which works out to 8.51 bits per weight, almost exactly what llama.cpp's Q8_0 format costs: every block of 32 one-byte weights carries a two-byte scale, so 8.5 bits each. [13] [9] Unsloth's memory figures, 223 GB at 1 bit and 810 GB at 8 bits, sit 6 to 9 GB above these file sizes. When you plan hardware, work from the file size and the memory guidance, not the label.

How the 27B holds a 256K-token conversation

Besides the weights, a model needs memory for its KV cache: the keys and values that attention layers store for every earlier token, so each new token can look back at them. It grows with every token, and the KV cache guide shows how to price it from a model's config.json. In a conventional model, the cache is what stops long context from fitting. Qwen3-32B, an all-attention model from 2025, stores 256 KiB per token; if it could hold 262,144 tokens, its cache alone would be 64 GiB. [15]

Qwen3.8-27B's configuration describes something else. Of its 64 layers, only 16, every fourth one, are full-attention layers with a KV cache. The other 48 are Gated DeltaNet layers, a kind of linear attention that keeps a fixed-size memory instead of a growing record. [8] [16]

KV per token = 2 (key and value)
             × 16 layers × 4 KV heads
             × 256 values × 2 bytes
             = 65,536 bytes = 64 KiB

At 262,144 tokens:
  16 GiB   at 16 bits
  8.5 GiB  as q8_0
  4.5 GiB  as q4_0

q8_0 and q4_0 are llama.cpp's 8-bit and 4-bit cache formats; counting their scales, they store 8.5 and 4.5 bits per value. [9] The Gated DeltaNet layers add a fixed 144 MiB per conversation, whether it is ten tokens long or 262,144: 48 layers, each holding 48 matrices of 128 × 128 values at 4 bytes. [8] The same attention shapes in every layer would need 64 GiB at full context; the hybrid needs a quarter of that. The rest comes down to the cache's precision. Next to a 17 GB 4-bit file, each halving of cache precision buys a doubling of context in about the same memory:

A 17 GB file is 15.8 GiB, and a card sold as 24 GB holds 24 GiB. The DeltaNet memory and the runtime's working buffers come out of what remains; in the one real full-context run described next, only 515 MiB was left. Lower-precision caches also cost some accuracy, which the KV guide covers.

That is how a post on Hacker News could report "Qwen3.8 27B at 256K: 50 TPS on a 24 GB GPU." It used a custom mixed file averaging 5 bits per weight, mostly in NVIDIA's 4-bit NVFP4 format (16,321 MiB), a 4-bit cache, a patched llama.cpp build and a second GPU for the vision component, and it used 23,952 of the card's 24,467 MiB. [4] The author also cut llama.cpp's saved copies of the DeltaNet memory from the default 32 to 4 (--ctx-checkpoints 4) to free space. The 50 tokens per second was the average of ten runs on the author's production workload, with the model's own draft head proposing several tokens at a time. With the 256K context actually full, generation ran at 12.6 tokens per second, because each new token also has to attend over the whole cache. [4]

The numbers pass the bandwidth test. The card moves 432 GB/s at peak, and producing one token without drafting means reading the 17.1 GB file once, a ceiling of about 25 tokens per second. The author measured 21.2 without drafting and 59.5 with it in a head-to-head run. [4] Drafting gets past that ceiling because one pass over the weights checks several proposed tokens at once; the bandwidth guide explains how. The author also did not stop at a successful load, and filled the context with a 261,500-token input before generating from it.

Why not make every layer linear?

Because a fixed-size memory has to compress, and compression loses exact details. Katharopoulos and colleagues showed in 2020 that linear attention can run like a recurrent network, carrying a fixed-size summary from token to token, so its memory stays flat however long the text gets. [17] The cost showed up when researchers measured recall. The Zoology study traced 82 percent of the quality gap between attention and gated-convolution models, an earlier family of efficient designs, to recalling something that appeared earlier in the text, although such tokens were only 6.4 percent of the training text. [18] The Based paper proved that exact recall over inputs of any length needs memory that grows with the input. [19] Gated DeltaNet uses its fixed memory more selectively, overwriting a single stored association or clearing the memory quickly when the topic changes, but its authors still got their best recall from hybrids that mixed attention back in. [16]

Qwen's report on the Flash-Next architecture, which uses the same three-to-one layout, gives that reason for it: the DeltaNet layers compress everything read so far into a fixed-size state, and one full-attention layer in four keeps the direct token-by-token lookup that no fixed-size memory reproduces exactly. In a controlled comparison on 25-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts test models (3 billion active), the DeltaNet hybrid beat an all-attention model on 8 of 9 benchmarks, averaging 53.81 against 49.87. The authors note that this does not show which component caused each gain. [20]

[OPINION] For long-context work, I would compare config files before parameter counts. Two models of similar size can need 16 GiB or 64 GiB for the same conversation, and only the config tells you which. If your work depends on pulling exact details out of very long documents, test that on your own files, since exact recall is what a compressed memory gives up first.

Reasoning effort: the default trades time for accuracy

Both Qwen3.8 models think before they answer: they write out working, which most chat apps fold away, and then the reply. A setting called reasoning effort controls how much, with three levels: low, medium and xhigh, the default. [8] The levels are instructions, not a limit on thinking length. Qwen's chat template adds a short instruction to the system prompt at xhigh, asking the model to think carefully, check key assumptions and weigh alternatives; a different one at low, asking it to keep its thinking brief; and nothing at medium. [8] Qwen3.8 also keeps earlier turns' reasoning in the conversation by default, so over a long session the thinking takes up KV cache as well as time. [8]

The complaints about xhigh are mostly about time, and partly about the model doing more than it was asked. Simon Willison's review called the 27B excellent but said it "defaults to wildly overthinking things." [21] His first attempt at a test drawing, an SVG of a pelican riding a bicycle, took 21 minutes and 22,276 tokens of reasoning; with reasoning off, the same prompt took 137 seconds. The thinking also used up LM Studio's default 8,192-token context on ordinary questions until Willison raised the limit. [21] A later post shared a modified chat template that makes medium the default. [22]

Piotr Migdał of Quesma measured what the time buys, running a 17 GB 4-bit file at each level. On GPQA Diamond, 198 graduate-level science questions, it scored 90.4 percent at xhigh and 83.3 percent at medium. On IFBench, which checks whether a model follows precise instructions, it scored 81.0 percent at xhigh and 62.7 percent at medium. The full-precision model showed similar drops, and Quesma's 95 percent confidence ranges overlap for GPQA but not for IFBench. [11] Qwen's model card adds a warning for agents: lower effort can make a multi-turn task slower overall, because thin analysis can lead to more failures and retries. [8]

Research on reasoning models explains why the default still feels wasteful. Chen and colleagues asked models what 2 plus 3 is. GPT-4o answered in 7 tokens. QwQ-32B-Preview, an earlier Qwen reasoning model, used 901: it solved the problem 13 times, though its first attempt had the answer after 39 tokens. Across two math test sets, when these models got a problem right, their first attempt already held the answer 89 to 97 percent of the time. [23] A study of coding agents found the other side of the trade on real GitHub issues: OpenAI's o1 solved 29.1 percent at high effort and 21.0 percent at low effort, and high effort cost 3.5 times as much. [24] Taken together, these results show the extra thinking paying off on hard science questions, strict instructions and real coding work, and mostly re-checking finished answers on simple arithmetic.

You can set the level per request. In llama.cpp's server (builds from 14 August on) and in vLLM, send "reasoning_effort": "medium" with the request; Qwen's model card shows the same field for SGLang. In Ollama, send "think": "medium". [25] [8] To turn thinking off, send "reasoning_effort": "none" to llama.cpp or vLLM, or "think": false to Ollama. [25] To change the default for a whole llama.cpp server:

llama-server -m Qwen3.8-27B-UD-Q4_K_M.gguf \
  --chat-template-kwargs '{"reasoning_effort":"medium"}'

On Windows, PowerShell 7.3 and later accept that single-quoted form unchanged. The older Windows PowerShell 5.1 needs '{\"reasoning_effort\":\"medium\"}', and Command Prompt needs "{\"reasoning_effort\":\"medium\"}". [26] Qwen's own template accepts only the three levels, so "high" fails with an error in vLLM and with ggml-org's llama.cpp files, while Unsloth's files and Ollama quietly treat it as xhigh. [8] [10] [25]

GLM-5.3 goes further in the same direction. Its levels are low, high and max, it defaults to max, and according to Unsloth and Z.AI its thinking cannot be switched off. [1] [2] [27]

[OPINION] I would keep xhigh for work that is hard to check, such as a subtle bug or a strict output format, and use medium or low for quick questions whose answers I will read anyway. Quesma's instruction-following drop at medium is why I would not make it the blanket default, as the community template does.

What each license lets you do

Open weights mean you can download the files. The license says what you may do with them, and the four models in this bulletin come with four different sets of terms. What follows is my reading of each license, not legal advice.

Licenses as of 31 August 2026
ModelLicenseIn short
Qwen3.8-27BApache-2.0Use, change, sell and host it, commercially or not. Keep the license and notices, and mark files you change. [8]
Qwen3.8-Flash-NextQwen Community License 1.0Broad use, commercial included, with two conditions. Keep the copyright and license notice, and any commercial product with more than 100 million monthly active users or US$20 million in monthly revenue must show the model's name prominently in its interface. If you or any affiliate runs a model-as-a-service or AI work-assistant business, you need a separate license from Qwen before any commercial use of the model, whatever your size. [28]
GLM-5.3GLM-5.3 License (Z.AI)An MIT-style grant (keep the notices, follow applicable law) with one added condition: if the licensee or an affiliate runs a model-as-a-service business and the group's revenue tops US$10 billion in any 12 months, it must pass Z.AI's security review before any commercial use. [29]
GLM-5.3-FlashMITUse it for anything; keep the copyright and license notice. [30]

The two custom licenses define a model-as-a-service business almost the same way: giving other people inference or fine-tuning access, such as an API, in which they meaningfully control the inputs, settings or training data. Neither counts passing requests on to a model someone else hosts. GLM-5.3's license also excludes end-user products where the model only powers specific features; the Qwen license has no such exclusion. [28] [29] Qwen's "AI work assistant" means a standalone product mainly for AI-assisted coding or office work; single-purpose tools, assistants for other fields and AI features inside other products are excluded. Purely internal use is exempt from the whole condition, as long as neither the model nor its outputs reach anyone outside the company. [28] The separate-license condition is the one most likely to catch a small team, because it applies at any size.

The GLM-5.3 license is also a change from its predecessor. Z.AI describes GLM-5.3 as the same base model as GLM-5.2, improved by further training, and GLM-5.2 was released under plain MIT. [27] [31] The new clause reaches only groups with more than US$10 billion in revenue that also run a model-as-a-service business.

Where each model runs

Qwen3.8-27B runs in llama.cpp builds from mid-August onward; Quesma found that earlier builds did not work. [11] Ollama lists it as qwen3.8 (version 0.32.12 or later), with a 4-bit default tag that includes the model's draft head, and vLLM has a recipe for it. [32] [33] For code, stay at 4 bits or above. Quesma ran Terminal-Bench 2.1, an agentic coding benchmark of 89 tasks carried out in a terminal: the 17 GB 4-bit file solved 63, the same as the full model, and the 10.7 GB 2-bit file solved 57. The smallest 1-bit file scored below the 25 percent that guessing earns on GPQA Diamond's four-option questions, and returned an empty answer about half the time or more. [11] On a Mac Studio with an M3 Ultra, one report measured 14 tokens per second from Ollama's 4-bit build. [34]

Qwen3.8-Flash-Next uses the same three-to-one hybrid as the 27B, with 36 DeltaNet layers and 12 sparse-attention layers (attention that reads only a selected subset of earlier tokens), and 125 billion parameters of which 6 billion are active per token. It also carries a 51-billion-parameter lookup table for common two- and three-token sequences, which runtimes can keep in system memory. [7] [35] It launched on 26 August with same-day SGLang support and a vLLM recipe, and llama.cpp added it the next day, in release b10660. [35] [33] [36] Unsloth's GGUF files, the format llama.cpp and Ollama load, are community conversions, not Qwen's own. [12]

GLM-5.3 shipped with a vLLM recipe (version 0.28.0 or later; its 8-bit FP8 weights need eight H200-class GPUs) and an SGLang guide, and mainline llama.cpp already supported its architecture, so Unsloth's GGUF files run on a current build. [1] [33] [2]

GLM-5.3-Flash, a sibling with 320 billion parameters (18 billion active), is MIT-licensed. Unsloth's smallest version needs 100 GB of memory, and on 31 August mainline llama.cpp did not support it. [30] [37]

FAQ

Can I run GLM-5.3 on a 24 GB card with a lot of system RAM?

Only if the two add up to about 223 GB, because Unsloth's figure counts GPU memory and system RAM together. [2] Expect it to be slow. Each token uses about 40 billion parameters, but not at the file's 2.3-bit average: the parts every token passes through (attention, the shared expert, the router) are stored at about 6 bits or more, so each token reads about 21 GB of the 1-bit file. [13] If all of that comes from two-channel DDR5 at about 90 GB/s, which is a best case, the ceiling is about 4 tokens per second. Keeping those always-used parts, about 15 GB, on the 24 GB card leaves roughly 6 GB of experts per token to stream from RAM, which raises the ceiling to roughly 12 tokens per second before real-world losses. The bandwidth guide explains the method.

Should I switch from the 27B to Flash-Next?

Not on this evidence. Flash-Next scores the same as or lower than the 27B on all nine Pulse dimensions, needs at least 75 GB of memory (Unsloth recommends a 96 GB machine), and your use must fit the Qwen Community License. Its grade is a launch-week grade, so check a later Pulse. [5] [12] [28]

Why is the 27B only a B− if it is the pick?

The composite comes from nine dimensions, not just fit. The 27B scores highest on Value (6.5); Flow sits at 4, which the pack puts down to the xhigh default, a setting you can change per request. [5]

Is GLM-5.3 MIT-licensed, like GLM-5.3-Flash and GLM-5.2?

No. It has its own license: an MIT-style grant plus a security-review condition for groups with more than US$10 billion in revenue that run a model-as-a-service business. Everyone else may use it commercially, keeping the notices. [29]

Will a smaller version of the 27B work on a 16 GB card?

Unsloth's 3-bit versions need 12 to 14 GB, so they fit. [3] Quesma's tests found the 2-bit file usable but noticeably weaker at coding: its 10.7 GB file solved 57 of 89 Terminal-Bench 2.1 tasks, against 63 for the 4-bit and full versions. Below that, quality collapsed, and Unsloth itself advises against 1-bit files for agents and tool calling. [11] [3]
