Local RAG on 16 GB: The Retrieval Tax

Updated: August 20, 2026

Embedding weights and retrieved chunks are not free context. On a 16 GB card they are a rent you pay in VRAM, and they can cost more than the quality they buy.

A home RAG stack looks cheap until you itemize it. The generator is only the first resident. The embedder wants a seat. The index wants RAM. Every retrieved chunk becomes key/value state in the generator, which is the same rent the VRAM guide already charges for long chats. On 16 GB, dated 2026-08-20, that rent is the difference between a 14B that reads three grep hits and an 8B that confidently answers from the wrong page.

Dated decision · 2026-08-20 · 16 GB

Spend the card on the generator unless the corpus is unstructured prose that lexical search cannot hit. If the files have unique tokens — function names, error strings, headings, ticket IDs — run a 14B at Q4_K_M and retrieve with ripgrep or a tight BM25 index. If you do need dense retrieval, pay for a small embedder and short chunks, not a mid-size embedder plus five long pages. The named tradeoff is generator quality versus retrieval machinery. Both stacks fit 16 GB. They do not buy the same answer.

Scientific Plate VII · Reading Room Card · Ad-free

The Reading Room

You do not carry the library to the desk. You request volumes. The desk is the context window. The volumes that arrive still occupy it. That occupancy is not a metaphor for “extra prompt text.” It is KV cache: key and value tensors written once per retrieved token, per layer, and reread on every later decode step.

The librarian is the retriever. The reader is the generator. The card in this plate is only the spine: request, occupancy, rent. House rule: the plate header stays ad-free. The arithmetic and the failure modes are the rest of the page, not a dump of the plate.

Ad-free · Read before the budget

Where the Metaphor Bends

  • A reading room assumes a competent librarian. Dense retrieval returns nearest neighbors in embedding space. Nearest is not “this answers the question.” Semantic near and task-relevant part company on homonyms, boilerplate, and adjacent sections of the same manual.
  • Chunks are not volumes. A 2,048-token slice is a torn signature. The sentence that names the API can sit in chunk 17; the sentence that lists the breaking change can sit in chunk 18. The desk then holds a confident half.
  • An empty desk still produces a letter. If top-k comes back empty or near-empty, the generator answers from parametric memory and still sounds grounded. Silence in the index is not an error message in the UI.
  • The stacks live in different rooms. HNSW graph and dense vectors usually sit in system RAM. Embedder weights and generator KV sit in VRAM. Adding “the index is only 200 MB” does not pay the retrieved-token bill on the GPU.
  • A better librarian does not enlarge the reader. Swapping a 137M embedder for a 4B embedder can move MTEB points. It does not turn an 8B generator into a 14B generator. On 16 GB that swap is often the reason the 14B no longer fits.

Why retrieval is a VRAM tax, not a free upgrade

Lewis, Perez, Piktus, and colleagues named the pattern in 2020: a parametric generator plus a non-parametric memory, queried at inference, with retrieved passages concatenated into the conditioning context. Their paper is arXiv:2005.11401, Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks. The home-lab translation is narrower than the paper. You are not fine-tuning BART on Wikipedia. You are keeping two models resident, or sequencing them, on one consumer card, and stuffing the winner’s context with whatever the index returned.

Fan, Ding, Ning, and colleagues survey that marriage of retrievers and LLMs as RA-LLMs — architectures, training, applications — in arXiv:2405.06211 (KDD 2024). Wang, Wang, Gao, and colleagues, arXiv:2407.01219, treat the same pipeline as a stack of replaceable modules: chunk, embed, store, retrieve, rerank, pack, generate. Both papers assume the interesting constraint is quality or latency. On a 16 GB box the interesting constraint is occupancy. Every module that is “just another model” is another resident. Every passage that is “just more context” is more KV.

The catalog already points 16 GB operators at a 14B-class generator — Phi-4 at about 11 GB Q4, Qwen3-14B at about 10.7 GB Q4. Those chips are catalog estimates, not first-party measurements, and they assume the community default Q4_K_M. The moment you also load a mid-size embedder and five long chunks, you have spent the headroom that made the 14B comfortable. You did not buy a smarter 8B. You bought an 8B plus a second model plus a longer notebook.

That is the tax. It has three invoices, and they do not itemize themselves in the UI:

  • Embedder weights. Resident in VRAM if you embed on the GPU, or a load/unload dance that hurts first-token latency. A 137M encoder is a rounding error. A 4B encoder is a second small LLM.
  • Index RAM. Dense vectors plus an HNSW graph. Usually system memory, not VRAM — until you put FAISS on the GPU and discover the graph bought a GPU seat too.
  • Retrieved-token KV. Once the generator sees a chunk, that chunk is conversation. It pays the same bytes-per-token as a user message of equal length. Five 2,048-token chunks are 10,240 tokens of cache before the question is interesting.

The pipeline, with a local cost on each step

Write the pipeline as five verbs. Each verb has a bill. Skip the bill and you will “add RAG” the way people add 128K context: it works in the demo and starves the card in the week that matters.

1. Chunk

Local cost: token count per slice, and the amputation risk at every cut. A chunker is a scissors. Overlap is a second copy of the same sentences, paid twice at retrieve time if both slices rank. There is no free overlap. Wang et al. (2024) treat chunking as a module you actually have to choose; the home rule is smaller than their lab sweep. Prefer 256 to 512 tokens on technical notes. Treat 2,048 as “I ingested files, not passages.” Five files of 2,048 tokens is a context strategy, not a retrieval strategy.

The hidden cost is structural. Markdown headings, function bodies, and definition/value pairs do not respect a fixed window. If the window cuts between “the default is” and “4096,” the embedder indexes a half-sentence and the generator never sees the number. That failure is first-class later on this page. It starts here, at the scissors.

2. Embed

Local cost: embedder VRAM plus encode-time compute. Two official cards, both HTTP 200 on 2026-08-20, bound the range this page will use:

  • nomic-ai/nomic-embed-text-v1.5 — Hub card license Apache-2.0. Safetensors parameter count 136,731,648. Card table lists sequence length 8192 and embedding width 768, with Matryoshka widths down to 64. Card requires a task prefix (search_document: / search_query:). FP16 weights calculate to 136,731,648 × 2 bytes = 273,463,296 bytes = 0.255 GiB, calculated.
  • Qwen/Qwen3-Embedding-4B — Hub card license Apache-2.0. Safetensors parameter count 4,021,774,336. Official config.json (HTTP 200): 36 layers, 32 query heads, 8 KV heads, head_dim 128. At the site’s Q4_K_M 4.8-bit convention this is 4,021,774,336 × 4.8 / 8 = 2,413,064,602 bytes = 2.25 GiB weights-only, calculated. The catalog shorthand Params_B × 0.5 would print 2.01 GB. The catalog’s Qwen3-Embedding row spans 0.6B / 4B / 8B at a chip of 0.5–7 GB; that chip is a catalog figure, not a first-party measurement.

The 4B is what this page means by mid-size: large enough that you feel it on 16 GB, small enough that people load it “for quality” without doing the arithmetic. The 8B sibling in that catalog row is a second 8B. Do not run two 8Bs on 16 GB and call it a retrieval upgrade.

Encode cost is mostly CPU or GPU time at ingest, then a smaller bill at query time. Ingest is a one-time tax unless the corpus churns. Query encode of a 32-token question is cheap. The resident weights are the part that steals the 14B’s chair.

3. Index (HNSW / ANN)

Local cost: system RAM for vectors plus graph, or VRAM if you force the index onto the GPU. Malkov and Yashunin’s Hierarchical Navigable Small World graphs are the usual ANN structure in local stacks. Paper: arXiv:1603.09320, later IEEE Transactions on Pattern Analysis and Machine Intelligence, 10.1109/TPAMI.2018.2889473. The structure is a layered proximity graph. Search starts at a coarse layer and descends. You pay for every stored vector and for the neighbor lists that make the hop possible.

Worked RAM, 50,000 chunks, 768-d float32 (the nomic v1.5 width), calculated:

  • Vectors: 50,000 × 768 × 4 = 153,600,000 bytes = 0.143 GiB.
  • Graph sketch: 50,000 nodes × M = 16 neighbors × 8-byte ids × 1.5 for the extra layers ≈ 9,600,000 bytes = 0.009 GiB.
  • Together ≈ 0.15 GiB system RAM, calculated. At 500,000 chunks the same sketch is about 1.5 GiB. At a million, about 3 GiB. Still usually cheaper than a 4B embedder — and still not free if the box is a 32 GB laptop that already holds the 14B in unified memory.

HNSW is approximate. That is the point of the paper. You traded exact nearest-neighbor for logarithmic hops. The tradeoff you actually feel is recall-at-k versus RAM and build time, not a guarantee that the right passage is in the top five. An exact scan of 50,000 × 768 is also cheap on a modern CPU. Do not deploy HNSW because a tutorial said “vector database.” Deploy it when brute force is the slow part. On a home corpus it often is not.

4. Retrieve

Local cost: top-k × chunk tokens, which become generator tokens. This is the invoice people miss. Retrieval quality is a ranking problem. Retrieval cost on a local LLM is a length problem. k = 5 at 2,048 tokens is 10,240 tokens. k = 3 at 384 tokens is 1,152 tokens. Same “I have RAG” checkbox. Nine times less notebook.

Rerankers (the catalog’s BGE reranker row) add a cross-encoder pass over the shortlist. They can cut silent-wrong-context if you then drop the losers. If you rerank and still paste all five chunks, you paid the reranker and kept the tax. The only retrieve setting that reduces KV is a smaller k or a shorter chunk, applied after the rerank, not before the story you tell yourself about quality.

5. Generate

Local cost: generator weights plus KV for prompt + retrieved tokens + completion. This is ordinary local inference, the same arithmetic as the VRAM guide and the quantization notes. RAG does not get a discount. The retrieved tokens are not “metadata.” They are sequence. Grouped-query attention makes the per-token rent smaller than older multi-head designs; it does not make 10k retrieved tokens free.

kv-cache.html is not on this site’s main tree as of 2026-08-20, so the formula on this page is the standard identity, labeled calculated:

bytes_per_token = 2 × n_layers × n_kv_heads × head_dim × bytes_per_element KV_bytes = bytes_per_token × seq_len × batch

The leading 2 is K and V. bytes_per_element is 2 for FP16/BF16 KV. Batch is 1 in every worked row below. Use KV heads, not query heads. Official configs opened for this page (all HTTP 200 on 2026-08-20):

  • Qwen3-8B (Qwen/Qwen3-8B): 36 layers, 8 KV heads, head_dim 128. 2 × 36 × 8 × 128 × 2 = 147,456 B/token = 144.00 KiB, calculated.
  • Qwen3-14B (Qwen/Qwen3-14B): 40 layers, 8 KV heads, head_dim 128. 2 × 40 × 8 × 128 × 2 = 163,840 B/token = 160.00 KiB, calculated.
  • Phi-4 (microsoft/phi-4): 40 layers, 10 KV heads, hidden 5120 so head_dim 128. 2 × 40 × 10 × 128 × 2 = 204,800 B/token = 200.00 KiB, calculated.

Five long chunks of 2,048 tokens on Qwen3-8B: 10,240 × 147,456 = 1,509,949,440 bytes = 1.41 GiB of retrieved-token KV, calculated. The same five chunks on Phi-4 would be 10,240 × 204,800 = 2,097,152,000 bytes = 1.95 GiB, calculated. Retrieval that “fits the 8B” is more expensive on the 14B you wish you had kept. That is not an argument for the 8B. It is an argument for shorter chunks if you insist on the 14B and RAG together — which, on 16 GB, you usually should not.

Worked 16 GB itemized budget

Two stacks. Same advertised card. Same day. Catalog VRAM chips are labeled as catalog figures — Hub cards for these generators do not print a GB occupancy, and this site did not measure them on a bench. KV and embedder-weight lines are calculated from official parameter counts and configs. Runtime reserve is a planning figure, not a measurement: 1.5 GB, in the same spirit as the 1.2× to 1.5× overhead the VRAM guide uses for cache and buffers when a chip is a range.

Usable memory is less than the sticker. A desktop compositor, a browser, and the CUDA context eat the edges. Treat 16 GB as about 15 GB of planning room. If a total lands between 14.5 and 16, it is tight, not comfortable.

Unified memory on a 16 GB Mac is the same decision with less isolation. The HNSW vectors and the generator weights compete for one pool. A 0.15 GiB index is still small. A 4B embedder plus an 8B generator plus 10k tokens of KV is how a “it loaded” session starts swapping. The stacks page already treats 16–24 GB unified as 7B/8B comfortable and 14B workable. Adding RAG does not raise that tier. It lowers it.

When the tax is worth paying

The decision is not “RAG is a scam.” The decision is “do not pay 14B prices in machinery to keep an 8B.” Pay the tax when the alternative retrieve cannot see the answer.

  • Pay it for unstructured prose: meeting notes, wiki dumps, email archives, scanned-to-text manuals where the query and the passage share meaning and not tokens. ripgrep of “how do we handle refunds” will miss a paragraph that never uses the word refund.
  • Pay it when the corpus is multilingual and the queries are not. nomic-embed-text-v2-moe’s card (HTTP 200, Apache-2.0, 475M total / 305M active, 512-token max sequence) exists for that job. It is still a small model. It is not a reason to load Qwen3-Embedding-8B beside an 8B chat model on 16 GB.
  • Do not pay it for a source tree, a log directory, or a notes vault that already has titles and identifiers. Unique strings are a gift. ripgrep of an error code will beat a 4B embedder that ranked the troubleshooting overview because both mention “timeout.”
  • Do not pay it to “ground” a model that already knows the public fact. RAG on a general encyclopedia, locally, is how you spend VRAM to retrieve a worse copy of Wikipedia than the 14B already compressed into its weights.
  • Hybrid is the adult move. Lexical first. Dense only on the miss. That is two retrieve paths and one generator. The embedder can stay on CPU. The 14B stays on the GPU. You pay encode latency on the hard queries, not a permanent 2.25 GiB lease.

Wang et al. (2024) spend a paper asking which module combination wins on quality and delay. Their default-looking RAG is already a pipeline, not a single embedding call. The local operator’s version of that result is smaller: most home quality failures are retrieve failures, and most home VRAM failures are “I left k and chunk length at the tutorial defaults.” Fix those before you buy a bigger embedder.

Failure modes, first-class

Cloud RAG tutorials treat these as footnotes under “limitations.” On a local card they are the reason the tax did not buy quality. Each one can produce a fluent answer. Fluency is not a test.

Silent wrong context

The index returns a passage that is about the right topic and the wrong claim. The generator, being an 8B instructed to use the context, repeats the claim with citations to your own files. You have built a machine for confident mis-summary of your own notes.

This is the default failure, not the rare one. Embedding space clusters by subject. A question about “the 2024 refund policy” is near “the 2022 refund policy,” “refund fraud,” and a draft you marked obsolete. Cosine does not read the year in the heading unless that year dominates the slice. If your chunker dropped the heading — see amputation — the 2022 policy is a near neighbor of the 2024 question and the reader cannot see the date that would have saved it.

What it looks like in the product: a green “grounded” badge, three chunk previews that mention the right nouns, and a number that is off. The operator who only spot-checks nouns will ship it.

What to do that is cheaper than a 4B embedder: put dates and titles inside every chunk (the heading belongs in the slice, not only in the filename); retrieve more than you paste, then drop; require a lexical filter when the query contains an identifier; show the operator the exact spans, not a similarity score. If you cannot see the span, you cannot catch the lie.

Chunk amputation

The scissors cut between the name and the value, the function and the return, the warning and the condition. Each half is a coherent English fragment. Neither half is the fact. The embedder indexes both. Retrieve can return the name-half because it matches the query nouns, and the value-half sits at rank 7, past your k = 5.

Long chunks make amputation rarer and KV more expensive. Short chunks make amputation common and KV cheap. That is the named tradeoff. It is not solved by “512 with 64 overlap” as a superstition. Overlap duplicates the cut region so both slices contain it — and duplicates the KV if both retrieve. Use overlap when your documents have definition/value pairs that straddle a fixed window. Do not use it as a default 20 percent tax on every note.

Code is the worst case. A 400-line file chunked at 512 tokens will separate a docstring from a body, a test from an assertion, a flag from the branch that reads it. ripgrep of the identifier returns the line and twenty neighbors. That is why Stack B is not a romantic preference for old tools. It is a chunker that respects tokens you already typed.

Markdown is the second-worst case. If you split on token count instead of headings, the ## line that scoped the section is in the previous slice. The embedder then sees a body that looks like every other body. Heading-aware splitters are not decoration. They are how you stop paying for slices that cannot be retrieved to the question that would have used the heading as a handle.

Empty retrieve

Top-k is never empty in most libraries. You asked for five neighbors; you get five neighbors. “Empty” here means empty of answers: the best cosine in the corpus is still a miss, or the query embedding landed in a hole because the prefix was wrong, the collection was stale, or the question used a name the notes never wrote.

nomic-embed-text-v1.5’s card is explicit: documents take search_document:, queries take search_query:. Drop the prefix and you have embedded two different tasks into one space. The neighbors you get are neighbors of a malformed query. That presents as empty retrieve even though k = 5 filled the prompt. The generator then does what generators do with irrelevant context: it ignores it, or it weaves it, and in both cases it sounds sure.

A true empty — filter by a score floor, get zero chunks — is healthier if the UI says so. Most home UIs do not have a floor. They have a slider for k. Raising k on a miss pastes more wrong context. That is how empty retrieve becomes silent wrong context. The cheap control is a minimum score and a visible “no passage, answering from weights” state. The expensive control is a 4B embedder. Use the cheap one first.

Semantic near is not relevant

This is the bends section, written as an operator bug. “Install CUDA” is near “CUDA programming model,” “CUDA samples,” and last year’s install note for a different distro. “Lease” in a contract corpus is near every paragraph that uses the word lease. Embedding models are trained to pull paraphrases together. They are not trained to pull “the passage that would change the answer” together. Those are different jobs. Fan et al. (2024) review architectures that try to close that gap with better retrievers, better training, better fusion. None of that is a 16 GB default. Your default is: if the query has a handle — a path, a symbol, a date, an ID — search the handle. Use cosine for the queries that are only a paraphrase.

A practical test: take ten questions you will actually ask. For each, print the retrieved spans before you generate. If you would not paste those spans into a 14B by hand, do not let the 8B see them. That test costs nothing in VRAM and catches more bad RAG than a larger embedder will.

An operator checklist for the 16 GB card

Work top to bottom. Stop when the answers are good. Each step is a refusal to spend VRAM on a story.

  • Name the corpus. Source tree, notes vault, or prose dump. If you cannot name it, you are not ready to chunk it.
  • Try lexical on ten real questions. If eight hits are usable, you do not have a RAG problem. You have a generator-size problem. Load the 14B.
  • If you still need dense: nomic-ai/nomic-embed-text-v1.5, Apache-2.0, prefixes on, 256–512 token heading-aware chunks, k = 3, score floor on, spans visible. Keep the embedder on CPU if the 14B is on the GPU.
  • Do not load Qwen3-Embedding-4B or 8B beside an 8B chat model on 16 GB unless you have measured a retrieve-only gain that the 14B cannot match with better hits. The catalog’s 0.5–7 GB chip is a warning label, not an invitation.
  • Cap retrieved tokens the way you cap chat history. A budget of 1,200 to 1,500 retrieved tokens is a policy. “Whatever top-5 returns” is not.
  • Re-embed when the files change, not on a schedule that ignores git. A stale index is empty retrieve with extra confidence.
  • If VRAM is tight, unload the embedder after ingest. Query encode of a short question is a brief reload or a CPU pass. A 2.25 GiB lease for a 32-token encode is how Stack A happens.

Hardware around the card still matters. The server guide is how you decide whether 16 GB is the machine or a waypoint. Recommended stacks is how the rest of the box is supposed to look. This page is only the retrieve-versus-generate fork on the card you already have.

Frequently asked questions

Should I spend 16 GB on retrieval or on a bigger generator?

On 2026-08-20, spend it on the generator when the files have unique tokens. Phi-4 at the catalog’s ~11 GB Q4, or Qwen3-14B at ~10.7 GB Q4, plus ripgrep, is the default 16 GB build on this site. Spend a slice on retrieval only when paraphrase is the access pattern. Even then, spend it on a 137M encoder and short chunks, not on a 4B encoder and five long pages.

How much VRAM does a local RAG stack add?

Itemized on this page: a mid-size 4B embedder at Q4_K_M is 2.25 GiB calculated; five 2,048-token chunks in a Qwen3-8B FP16 cache are 1.41 GiB calculated; nomic-embed-text-v1.5 at FP16 is 0.255 GiB calculated; three 384-token chunks in the same 8B cache are 0.16 GiB calculated. The index for 50k 768-d vectors is about 0.15 GiB of system RAM, calculated, if it stays on the CPU. Catalog generator chips (6–7 GB for Qwen3-8B, ~11 GB for Phi-4) are not first-party measurements.

Why do retrieved chunks cost VRAM after I already paid for the embedder?

Because they become sequence in the generator. The embedder’s job ends when the ids are chosen. The generator must attend to every pasted token on every later decode step, which is the KV identity 2 × n_layers × n_kv_heads × head_dim × bytes_per_element. Paying for the librarian does not pay the desk rent.

Is HNSW required for a home corpus?

No. Malkov and Yashunin designed it for large approximate search. Fifty thousand 768-d vectors are a few hundred milliseconds of brute force on a desktop CPU. Use HNSW when the corpus is large enough that the hops matter, or when the library you picked made it the default. It is a RAM-and-recall trade, not a quality blessing.

Can I run the 14B and a small embedder together on 16 GB?

On paper: Phi-4 ~11 GB catalog plus nomic-embed-text-v1.5 at 0.255 GiB plus 1.5 GB reserve plus a few hundred megabytes of tight-hit KV. That can fit if the catalog chip is not already a high-end 11. If the chip is already “~11 GB” including some context, add the embedder on CPU instead. Do not add Qwen3-Embedding-4B (2.25 GiB calculated) on the GPU next to Phi-4 and expect 2,048-token chunks. The card will tell you, late, in the form of an OOM that looks like a driver bug.

Does a bigger embedder fix wrong-but-plausible retrieval?

Sometimes it moves the right span from rank 8 to rank 3. It does not make cosine understand “obsolete” or “do not use.” Heading-in-chunk, lexical filters, a score floor, and visible spans fix more silent wrong context than a jump from 137M to 4B. If you have 24 GB, the better spend is still usually a 32B generator, not a larger encoder. See the VRAM guide for that tier.
